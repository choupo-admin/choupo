\section{What this guide is --- and what it is not}\label{sec:edu-what}

EduTools is the plane of Choupo's browser application where the
\emph{classical graphical methods} are constructed, most of them over
curves the engine computes: the McCabe--Thiele staircase, the Kremser
recovery curve, the $\varepsilon$--NTU chart, the Levenspiel plot, the
Van~Heerden ignition diagram, the pinch composite curves, and the rest.
Most tools draw a construction a student has met on paper on numbers a
real solver produced, and put the method's answer beside the engine's;
some work a closed form on the page, quote a cited source or a recorded
run, or start from values the reader sets.  Every result on a page says
which: it carries one of four origin marks --- \emph{engine},
\emph{page}, \emph{reference} or \emph{assumption} --- explained in a
legend above the tool.

\begin{keyidea}
This guide is a \textbf{lesson companion}, not a theory manual.  It says
what each construction is \emph{for}, what to turn, what to watch, and
what the picture is silent about.  It contains no derivations at all.
\end{keyidea}

That absence is deliberate and it is architectural.  Every equation behind
these tools already has exactly one home --- \file{docs/theoryGuide.pdf} ---
and every live tool declares, in its registry entry, the named destination
in that guide where its derivation lives.  A second document carrying the
same derivations would be a second home for the same statement, and within
one semester the two would disagree.  So this guide \emph{points}, and the
Theory Guide \emph{derives}.  When you want to know why
$\varepsilon = (1-e)/(1-C_r e)$, follow the pointer.  When you want to know
what a class will get wrong about it, you are in the right document.

\subsection*{The five questions each tool section answers}

Every tool documented here is written to the same five headings, in the
same order, because a lesson is more portable than a description:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The question} --- in one sentence a student would recognise
        from a problem sheet.
  \item \textbf{What to vary, what to watch} --- the specific knob that
        makes the lesson visible, and what should happen when you turn it.
  \item \textbf{The misconception the construction exposes} --- what
        students actually get wrong, not merely what is true.  This is the
        part worth reading twice.
  \item \textbf{What the picture does not say} --- the limits of the
        construction.  In this project that is treated as part of teaching
        it, never as an apology for it.
  \item \textbf{Where the derivation lives} --- a pointer into the Theory
        Guide, never a copy of it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Where the other material is}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Theory Guide} --- every derivation, every hypothesis, every
        worked example.  The authority for all physics.
  \item \textbf{Explorer Guide} --- the \emph{property surfaces}
        ($T$-$x$-$y$, $\gamma(x)$, $P^{\mathrm{sat}}$ scans).  The split
        criterion between the two planes is stated in the application
        itself: method-construction $\rightarrow$ EduTools,
        property-surface $\rightarrow$ Explorer.
  \item \textbf{Tutorials Guide} --- the runnable cases.  Every EduTools
        tool runs one of them; this guide names which.
  \item \textbf{User Guide} --- authoring cases on disk, which is where any
        finding from a tool should end up.
\end{itemize}

\section{How a tool feeds itself}\label{sec:edu-feeding}

\begin{whatyoulllearn}
Where the numbers on the screen come from, why a tool works with no case
open, and what a knob is actually allowed to change.
\end{whatyoulllearn}

\subsection{Opening a tool}

EduTools is a tab of its own.  The tool chooser leads the chrome row on
that tab; on a case tab showing EduTools in its body the chooser is one
slim row above the construction.  Either way the list is the same, and a
construction that is on the roadmap but not yet built is shown
\emph{disabled} rather than hidden --- the menu states the roadmap instead
of implying it.

Every tool is deep-linkable:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=shell]
?workspace=methods&tool=<id>
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The \code{methods} key is a contract and does not change with the
workspace's caption, so a link already in a student's notes goes on
working.  The tool identifiers are the ones listed in
\S\ref{sec:edu-tools} and \S\ref{sec:edu-stubs}.

Under the chooser sits a one-line caption of what the construction
teaches, and beside it a book icon that opens the Theory Guide \emph{at
this tool's own section}.  That icon is the pointer this whole guide is
built around: a tool that cannot name its derivation is not finished, and
a test in the application refuses an anchor the Theory Guide does not
define.

\subsection{Paths, and finding a tool}

Above the chooser sits one compact row, \emph{Path}.  A path is a reading
order over tools that already exist --- three ship: distillation,
thermodynamics and reaction engineering --- and choosing one shows its
steps as a numbered strip with the current step marked, a sentence saying
what that step adds, and Previous and Next buttons.  A path adds no page
and no panel: it only moves between the same tools, and its address,

\begin{lstlisting}[language=shell]
?workspace=methods&tool=<id>&path=<path id>
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
carries both, so a teacher can send a student the third step of a path as
one link.  Each tool also has a one-line blurb of at most twelve words,
which is what the public landing page prints large on its cards (the longer
description sits below it).  The landing page also has a text filter
over the tools, and its discipline shelves fold; without JavaScript it is
simply the full list.

\subsection{Classroom, or your own run}

Most tools default to \textbf{Classroom}: the tool clones a bundled
tutorial --- its \emph{witness} case --- writes your knob values into that
case's own dictionary files, and solves it in the browser with the
WebAssembly build of the same solver the command line runs.  Nothing needs
to be open, and nothing on disk is touched.

When the application's current run also carries the surfaces a tool reads,
a source toggle offers \textbf{Current run} beside the classroom witness.
A tool that a run cannot feed says so and stays empty; it never draws
something else wearing the same name.

\begin{keyidea}
A knob turns a \emph{declared scalar} in the witness's dictionary and
nothing else.  The engine then recomputes everything.  A knob that cannot
find its key fails loudly rather than silently doing nothing, and a knob
that would not reach the physics is deliberately absent --- each absence
recorded with its reason in the tool's own source.
\end{keyidea}

That last rule has teeth.  Several plausible-looking controls are missing
on purpose: a pressure that a liquid-phase rate law never reads would be a
placebo control; an integrator step count sitting beside physical knobs
invites reading numerics as physics; a kinetic pre-exponential that a
witness deliberately re-tuned would leave the tool teaching a case other
than the one it names.

\subsection{Why the picture can be trusted}

Three standing rules govern this plane, and they are worth telling a class
about, because they are the reason the construction and the simulator
cannot quietly diverge.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{No physics in the browser.}  Every curve is an engine
        column, an engine KPI, or an engine-written report file.  The
        browser draws.
  \item \textbf{Method geometry is the one exception, and it is
        labelled.}  A staircase, a trapezoid area, a textbook closed form,
        a heat cascade re-run over published curves --- these \emph{are}
        the method being taught, so a few tools own them.  Where that
        happens the tool says so on screen, and the engine's own number is
        the judge: agreement is verification, and a disagreement is a
        finding about the drawing, never a moved answer.
  \item \textbf{An absence stays an absence.}  A number the engine did not
        publish is not rendered as zero and not invented.  Solver
        advisories are lifted out of the run log and shown, because these
        tools have no log tab to bury them in.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{pitfall}
A tool that shows nothing is usually telling you something.  ``This run
publishes no Van~Heerden surface'' means the reactor you solved was
isothermal --- there is no generation-versus-removal diagram for it.  The
honest empty state is a diagnosis; read it before changing the tool.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Distillation: the McCabe--Thiele staircase}\label{sec:edu-mccabe}

\declaredanchor{ch:distillation}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{A binary feed of composition $x_F$ is to be split into a distillate
$x_D$ and a bottoms $x_W$.  At reflux ratio $R$ and feed quality $q$, how
many equilibrium stages does it take, and on which stage does the feed
enter?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Set a sharp split ($x_D = 0.95$, $x_W = 0.05$, $x_F = 0.5$), then drag
$R$ \emph{down} toward the red $R_{\min}$ tick on the slider.  Watch three
things at once: the staircase steps multiply, the \emph{approach to pinch}
bar fills and reddens, and at $R \le R_{\min}$ the construction stops
with a banner naming the pinch abscissa.  Then drag $R$ up and watch how
little the stage count changes once you are clear of the cliff.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{$R$} --- the primary knob.  The slider can be bound to
        $R$ directly or to $R/R_{\min}$; the second binding is the design
        lens (``$1.3\times$ minimum'') and makes the cliff a fixed
        landmark at $1.0$ instead of a number that moves.
  \item \textbf{$q$} --- rotates the $q$-line about $(x_F, x_F)$.  Watch
        the feed-stage index move, and watch $R_{\min}$ itself change:
        the same column, the same specifications, a different minimum
        reflux, because the feed arrived hotter or colder.
  \item \textbf{$E_{MV}$} --- the Murphree tray efficiency.  Below $1$ the
        staircase steps to a dashed \emph{pseudo}-curve and the count
        becomes a count of real trays.
  \item \textbf{The partial-condenser switch} --- adds exactly one
        equilibrium stage, and the readout says so.
  \item \textbf{The components, $\gamma$ model and $P$} --- these are the
        only settings that re-run the engine, because they are the only
        ones that change the equilibrium curve.  Everything else redraws
        instantly.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the equilibrium curve responds to the reflux ratio.}  It is
the commonest confusion in the whole subject, and it is usually invisible
because on paper the student redraws the entire figure each time.  Here the
curve is a frozen engine result and the staircase is pure geometry over it,
so turning $R$ at sixty frames a second while $y^{*}(x)$ does not so much
as flicker makes the point physically: \emph{reflux is an operating
decision; the equilibrium curve is a statement about the mixture}.  Change
the pressure or the activity model and the engine re-runs and the curve
moves --- which is the same lesson from the other side.
\end{pitfall}

Two further confusions the same picture settles:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{That $R_{\min}$ is a property of the column.}  It is the
        intersection of the $q$-line with the equilibrium curve --- feed
        composition, feed quality, product specifications and
        thermodynamics, and no equipment at all.  Turn $q$ and $R_{\min}$
        moves although nothing has been built or unbuilt.
  \item \textbf{That more reflux is better because it needs fewer
        stages.}  The chart shows only one half of that trade.  Stage
        count falls steeply just above $R_{\min}$ and then flattens; the
        vapour traffic $V = (R+1)D$, and with it the reboiler duty, the
        condenser duty and the column diameter, goes on rising linearly
        and is \emph{nowhere on this diagram}.  A student who optimises on
        the staircase alone will choose a column that cannot be paid for.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{The operating lines are straight because Constant Molar
        Overflow is assumed.}  The tool states this permanently under the
        chart rather than in a footnote.  CMO is an energy assumption
        (equimolar latent heats), and it is the one thing on the diagram
        that is not the real model.  The equilibrium curve, by contrast,
        is the real curve at that pressure --- not a constant-$\alpha$ toy.
  \item \textbf{No energy, no hydraulics, no diameter.}  There is no
        reboiler duty, no condenser duty, no pressure drop, no flooding
        check.  A column that is impossible to build converges perfectly
        well here.
  \item \textbf{Stages are not trays} unless $E_{MV} < 1$, and $E_{MV}$
        is a \emph{declared} number --- from a correlation, a chart, or
        plant data.  The engine will not invent it.
  \item \textbf{The azeotrope you see belongs to the declared model.}  If
        no curated binary-interaction pair covers the system, the tool
        announces that it is drawing \emph{ideal} mixing and that the
        diagram therefore cannot show an azeotrope at all.  Read that
        banner: an absent azeotrope may be a fact about the mixture or a
        fact about the data coverage, and only the banner tells you which.
  \item \textbf{Binary only.}  A third component has no axis here.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Distillation column, Wang-Henke
bubble-point} (\theorydest{ch:distillation}) --- the stage model, CMO, and
the rigorous column this construction is the hand method for.  The closed
forms for the two limits the staircase locates geometrically,
$N_{\min}$ and $R_{\min}$, are derived in \emph{Shortcut distillation:
Fenske--Underwood--Gilliland} (\theorydest{ch:fug}).  The Murphree
efficiency and its effective-$K$ treatment are in \emph{Tray hydraulics}
(\theorydest{ch:hydraulics}).  The staircase construction itself ---
horizontal to the operating line, vertical to the equilibrium curve --- is
drawn and explained in the absorber chapter
(\theorydest{ch:absorber}), where the same geometry serves a
counter-current cascade.}

\subsection{Equilibrium stages or a rate-based model?}\label{sec:edu-equilibrium-or-rate-based}

\declaredanchor{ch:ratebased}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=equilibrium-or-rate-based}.
\textit{\small Cases it runs:} \file{column11\_murphree} as shipped and with
four declared numbers rewritten, and the three tray-efficiency witnesses
\file{trayEfficiency01\_yanagi14}, \file{trayEfficiency05\_chanFair\_yanagi14}
and \file{trayEfficiency06\_syedaChuang\_yanagi14}, and, for the closing
section on the HETP, the rate-based packed bed \file{ratebased01\_schulz\_0p10bar}
run once without its F-factor sweep (all in
\file{tutorials/steady/distillation/}).

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have to design or rate a column: is an equilibrium-stage model with one
efficiency enough, or do I need a rate-based model, and how wrong can the
simple one be?}

The page states first what the engine has: a declared Murphree efficiency on
the Wang--Henke column, a computed tray efficiency for a binary at total
reflux, and one rate-based column, for a binary packed bed at total reflux
only (\code{model rateBased;}).  Every recommendation on the page is either
demonstrated by an engine run on the page or read in a paper the page lists,
and the closing decision table names the origin of every row.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Demonstration~1 runs one benzene--toluene column at seven efficiencies, from
0.4 to 1.0, twice: as the tutorial ships it (a few per cent of toluene in the
distillate) and taller at higher reflux (parts per million).  Going from an
efficiency of 0.7 to 0.6 multiplies the first impurity by about 1.2 and the
second by about 4.  Then count the trays a 10~ppm target needs at three
efficiencies: 27 real trays at 1.0, 37 at 0.7, 63 at 0.4.  Demonstration~2
runs three published correlations on one measured sieve tray: at an F-factor
of 1.5 they span about 28 points, while the measured efficiency stays between
63 and 74\,\% over the normal range of load.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a column built from equilibrium stages and one efficiency is safe
because the separation is easy.}  On a trace specification the efficiency acts
once per tray and the error compounds; the uncertain number is the efficiency
itself, which correlations for the same tray do not agree on.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{Where HETP fails}

The last section of the page is about the one number a packed bed is usually
sized with.  \emph{The HETP is not a property of the packing alone.}  For a
binary with a straight equilibrium line and a straight operating line,
$\mathrm{HETP} = H_{OG}\,\ln\lambda/(\lambda-1)$ with $\lambda = mV/L$
(Seader, Henley and Roper, 4th ed., Eqs.~7-80, 7-86 and 7-87; the engine's own
relation is \file{PackingDelft.cpp:131}).  A slider on $\lambda$ draws the
factor between HETP and $H_{OG}$: it is one at $\lambda=1$ and moves away on
either side, so the same packing has a different HETP where the flows or the
equilibrium slope differ.  The book's Example~7.7 ($\lambda=0.76$, $H_{OG}=1.52$~ft,
HETP $=1.74$~ft) is recomputed on the page.  The second panel runs the
rate-based bed once and reads the segment profile the engine publishes
($m$, $V$, $L$, $H_{OG}$ in each of 100 segments): $\lambda$ goes from about
0.85 at the bottom to about 1.11 at the top, so it crosses one inside the bed;
$H_{OG}$ rises along the bed while the local HETP computed from the relation
falls, by about a tenth of its mean, so one HETP for the whole bed is an average
over a bed that works differently at its ends.  The local HETP is the page's
construction on the engine's numbers; the engine prints none.  Third, for a
mixture of more than two components the relation read one component at a time
gives each component its own $\lambda_i = m_iV/L$ and its own HETP, so no single
HETP exists; the engine's rate-based column is binary, so this is stated and not
run.  The page asks for a guess before it shows the bed.  It has no quiz.

\subsubsection*{What the page takes from references}

Four open conference papers, each read for the page, and four papers read by
the project's coordinating session and not opened for the page: two commercial H$_2$S strippers where one
assumed efficiency put the bottoms impurity two orders of magnitude below the
plant and the computed component efficiencies varied from about 20 to 60\,\%
along the column (Taylor, Wilkinson and Kooijman, 2010); a quaternary
azeotropic mixture whose measured trajectories crossed a distillation boundary
that an equilibrium-stage model cannot cross (Springer, van der Molen and
Krishna, 2002); a packed column at total reflux, near the boundary of the
2-propanol and water azeotrope, whose reboiler filled with water where the
equilibrium-stage model predicted 2-propanol, so that model got the product
wrong, not a percentage (Pelkonen, G\'orak, Ohligschl\"ager and Kaesemann,
2001); reactive absorption in sour gas treatment,
where an equilibrium model got the H$_2$S over CO$_2$ selectivity wrong
(Thiele et al., 2006); and an industrial dehexaniser study on how efficiencies
vary by component and tray (Jakobsson et al., 2002).  From the three further papers
read by the coordinating session the page takes: that with three or more
components the component efficiencies are almost always unequal and can exceed
one or fall below zero (Krishnamurthy and Taylor, 1985; Taylor and Krishna,
2000); that for a reactive column equilibrium stages serve preliminary design
while final design, control and the dynamics call for the nonequilibrium model
(Taylor and Krishna, 2000); and, in the costs, what a rate-based model needs
(hardware data, mass-transfer and hydrodynamic parameters, diffusion
coefficients, viscosity, surface tension), that the tray flow model alone moves
the transfer rate by up to about 20\,\%, and the one paper's own count of the
size of the problem (70 against 160 equations for ten stages and three
components, 550 against no fewer than 1\,300 for fifty stages and five), which
its authors call a considerable difference but a minor drawback (Krishnamurthy
and Taylor, 1985; Taylor and Kooijman, 2014).  The trace specification is the
page's own finding from demonstration~1: none of the papers names it as a
reason to choose a rate-based model.

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

No rate-based model of a tray, a feed or three or more components; no
component efficiencies that differ, and no run of the per-component HETP; demonstration~1
is a near-ideal binary on constant molar overflow, with the trace column's
numbers and the targets chosen by the page.  The measured tray efficiencies are
the tree's transcription, not re-read for the page.  A review often cited for
these recommendations (Real-world modeling of distillation, 2003) could not be
opened, so the list of situations is incomplete.  The measured tables and the
dropped candidates are in \file{docs/design/equilibrium-or-rate-based.md}.

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Columns from their internals: total reflux,
computed tray efficiency, packed beds} (\theorydest{ch:ratebased}) for the
computed efficiency, and \emph{Distillation column, Wang-Henke bubble-point}
(\theorydest{ch:distillation}) for the column and its declared Murphree
efficiency.}

\subsection{Absorption: the Kremser shortcut, judged}\label{sec:edu-kremser}

\declaredanchor{ch:absorber}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{A gas carrying a soluble solute is washed counter-currently with a
lean solvent.  How much of the solute do $N$ stages recover --- and does
the one-line shortcut agree with what the column actually does?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

The construction has two halves and the lesson is in the gap between them.
The curve is the Kremser closed form, recovery against $N$ at the
absorption factor $A = L/(KV)$ the engine published.  The point is the
engine's own stagewise recovery for the column that was solved.  The chip
between them is the labelled deviation.

\begin{tryit}
Start from the witness (an ammonia/water absorber, six stages, both feeds
at $298.15$~K).  Now raise the ammonia in the gas feed, step by step, and
watch \emph{two} chips together: \code{dT\_rise} climbing as more heat of
absorption is released, and the deviation between the shortcut and the
column growing with it.  Then put the ammonia back and raise the solvent
flow instead: $A$ rises, recovery rises, and the deviation shrinks.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Stages $N$} --- the knob students reach for first.  Watch
        how quickly the curve flattens: past a handful of stages, more
        stages buy very little.
  \item \textbf{Solvent flow $L$} --- the knob that actually moves the
        answer, because it moves $A$.
  \item \textbf{Solute feed flow} --- the knob that breaks the shortcut,
        by making the column non-isothermal.
  \item \textbf{Feed temperatures} --- both of them, gas and solvent, with
        the \code{nonIsothermal} flag and \code{dT\_rise} as the readout.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the deviation is an error.}  A student who has only met the
closed form reads any gap between shortcut and simulator as a defect ---
a bad $K$, a loose tolerance, a bug.  It is neither.  Kremser assumes
\emph{one constant} $A$, which means a straight operating line and a
straight equilibrium line, which means an isothermal column.  Absorption
is exothermic.  The solvent heats as it descends, $K$ rises with
temperature, and $A = L/(KV)$ therefore \emph{falls} exactly where the
column is doing its work.  The engine re-evaluates $K$ stage by stage; the
shortcut cannot.  The gap is the assumption failing, and it is real.
\end{pitfall}

The direction matters as much as the size, and it is the part worth
labouring: for a soluble, exothermic solute the isothermal Kremser is
\emph{optimistic}.  It promises recovery the column will not deliver.  A
shortcut that erred the other way would merely be conservative; this one
sends you to site with a column that underperforms.

A second confusion, milder but persistent:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{That $A > 1$ settles the matter.}  $A$ is a ratio of two
        slopes, not a pass mark.  At $A$ barely above one, recovery
        approaches its ceiling so slowly that no realistic stage count
        gets there; at $A$ comfortably above one, a few stages do almost
        everything.  The curve makes the difference between those two
        situations obvious in a way that the inequality $A > 1$ never can.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{The $A$ on the chip is a single number for a column whose
        $K$ varies.}  It is evaluated at the feed temperature.  That is
        precisely why the two sides of the deviation chip are not the same
        calculation --- and precisely what makes the chip informative.
  \item \textbf{Absorbers only.}  A stripper publishes a stripping factor

        missing feature: the mirrored construction is a different lesson.
  \item \textbf{Stages are theoretical.}  No packing height, no HETP, no

        real plant regenerates it, and the regenerator's residual solute
        sets a floor on the recovery that no stage count can beat.  That
        loop is not on this diagram.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the transfer unit was invented: $Z = H_{OG}N_{OG}$}\label{sec:edu-transfer-unit}

\declaredanchor{sec:packed-bed}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=transfer-unit}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} \code{steady/distillation/ratebased01\_schulz\_0p10bar},
run in the browser by a button on the page, whose published $H_{OG}$, $m$, $V$,
$L$ profile and KPIs are read; steps 1 to 4 are the page's own arithmetic and
run no engine.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{My packed tower is too tall or too short.  How much of its height is the
fault of the separation and how much of the packing, and how does that connect
to the stages I counted on the McCabe--Thiele diagram?}

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Packed beds at total reflux}
(\theorydest{sec:packed-bed}): the transfer-unit equation
$\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}z = (y^*-y)/H_{OG}$, $H_{OG} = H_G + \lambda H_L$ and
$\mathrm{HETP} = H_{OG}\ln\lambda/(\lambda-1)$.  The page derives the last of
these from Colburn's equation and the Kremser count, for two straight lines.}

The page has two halves and says which one you are in.  In the \textbf{page}
half the height of a dilute absorber with straight lines is written as
$Z = H_{OG}N_{OG}$: the number of transfer units
\[
N_{OG} = \frac{\ln\!\left[(1-1/A)\,y_{\mathrm{in}}/y_{\mathrm{out}} + 1/A\right]}{1-1/A},
\qquad A = \frac{L}{mG},
\]
(Colburn's equation, Treybal Eq.~8.50) depends on the recovery and on $A$ only,
and the height of a unit $H_{OG} = G/(K_y a)$ on the packing and the flows.
Three sliders (the recovery, $A$, and $H_{OG}$) move the two halves
independently, and $N_{OG}$ is drawn against $A$ at the chosen recovery.  In the
\textbf{engine} half a button runs the shipped rate-based bed, and the page
takes two sums on what it published: $N_{OG} = \sum \Delta z/H_{OG,k}$ over the
segments, and the HETP that $H_{OG}$ and $\lambda = mV/L$ give segment by
segment, beside the KPIs \code{HETP} and \code{theoreticalStages\_bed}.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says}

The segment profile carries the \code{HOG} column
(\code{RateBasedSegment.cpp}:886) with $\lambda = m v/l$
(\code{RateBasedSegment.cpp}:456), and the packing models write the HETP
relation (\code{PackingRBF.cpp}:138--139, \code{PackingDelft.cpp}:131--132).
The page's test holds the closed form to a numerical integral of
$\mathrm{d}y/(y-y^*)$, and the HETP relation to the Kremser count.  The
statement that $G/(F_G a)$ is much more constant than $G$ or $F_G a$ is
Treybal's, after his Eq.~8.25; the paper that introduced the split is
Chilton and Colburn, Ind.\ Eng.\ Chem.\ \textbf{27} (1935) 255--260, which is
cited and not quoted.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Set the recovery to 0.99 and lower $A$ from 3 to 1: $N_{OG}$ rises to 99 and
is finite at $A = 1$.  Go on below 1: the number goes to infinity at $A = R$,
the liquid rate at which the leaving liquid would be in equilibrium with the
entering gas.  Then move $H_{OG}$ and watch only the height move.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the pole is at $A = 1$.}  It is at $A = R$.  And that HETP equals
$H_{OG}$: they are equal only at $\lambda = 1$; above it the HETP is the
smaller.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Straight lines, dilute solute, constant flows; a distillation at total reflux
is the only engine case, so there is no packed absorber to compare with, and no
curve of $H_{OG}$ against flow.  Plug flow: no axial mixing or maldistribution.

\subsection{One flux, many units: film coefficients, Henry's law and reaction in the film}\label{sec:edu-one-flux-many-units}

\declaredanchor{sec:rateBasedSegment}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=one-flux-many-units}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none --- every number on the page is the page's own
arithmetic, from the formula printed beside it; the engine is cited where it
uses one of the forms, never run.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have a film coefficient from a correlation, a Henry's-law constant from
a table and a flux to compute: which form is each one in, how do I get the form
my equation needs, and what changes when the gas reacts in the liquid?}

One interfacial flux is written with five film coefficients --- $k_y$, $k_c$,
$k_p$ in the gas, $k_x$, $k_L$ in the liquid, per unit area or, times $a$, per
unit volume --- that differ only in the driving force the flux is divided by;
a converter takes any one in any unit, with the temperature, the pressure and
the liquid's molar concentration, and prints every other with the step that
gives it.  A table sets the books' names side by side: the same letter means
different things in two books (Treybal's prime marks the equimolar
coefficient, Seader's the one for a stagnant component), and in two chapters of
one book (Seader's Henry constant is a solubility in chapter~3 and a volatility
in chapter~6).  The drift factor of a film that only the solute crosses is
derived from Taylor and Kooijman's film-model logarithm.  Henry's law is shown
in Sander's seven variants, solubilities and volatilities, with a converter and
the slope $m$ of $y = mx$; oxygen's tabulated value is set beside Choupo's own
oxygen record.  Overall coefficients are built as resistances in series, with
the share of each film; then Seader's SO$_2$ example, on a measured curved
equilibrium line, shows three slope choices against the exact interface, and a
straight Henry line through the origin missing the flux by more than 80\,\%.
The last step is a reaction in the liquid film: the Hatta number, Missen's
regimes, $E = \mathrm{Ha}/\tanh\mathrm{Ha}$, the instantaneous limit and van
Krevelen and Hoftijzer's approximation, drawn as $E$ against Ha on log axes.
Six short exercises close the page, each with its data, its source, a worked
solution and the answer, hidden until asked.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

The engine computes none of the page's numbers.  The page cites where it uses
the forms: the rate-based packed segment takes $k_G$ and $k_L$ from the packing
correlation in m/s, multiplies each by its phase's molar density (the $k_y =
c_G k_c$ conversion of the first step) and by the effective area and the
segment's volume, carries the bulk flow explicitly with low-flux coefficients,
and solves the interface rather than using an overall coefficient.  A Henry
record stores $p/x$ in Pa (Sander's $K_\mathrm{H}^{px}$, although the record's
convention names it \code{H\_xp}), carried in temperature by van~'t~Hoff's form,
and a Henry solute's K-value is that pressure over the total pressure.  Every
formula on the page was read in a filed source at the equation it names:
Treybal (1980) Eqs.~3.3--3.6, Table~3.1 and Eqs.~5.1--5.19; Seader et
al.\ (2016) Eqs.~3-179 to 3-216, Table~3.12, Example~3.18 and Eqs.~6-128 to
6-132; Sander (2015) Eqs.~1--16 and Tables~1--4 and~6; Taylor and Kooijman
(2014) Eqs.~3.44, 3.68 and 3.77--3.82; Missen et al.\ (1999) Sect.~9.2 and
Example~9-6.  The page's tests hold the conversions in both directions, Sander's
conversion factors, Seader's and Missen's printed results and every exercise
answer to the digits it prints.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
In the first converter, enter Seader's gas coefficient (0.040\,kmol/(h\,m$^2$\,kPa)
at 50\,\textdegree C and 2\,atm) and read $k_y = 8.1$\,kmol/(h\,m$^2$); then
raise the bulk mole fraction of A towards 0.5 and watch the stagnant-film
coefficient pull away from the low-flux one.  In the Henry converter pick
ammonia, then oxygen: the slope $m$ moves by about 4.7 orders of magnitude (from 0.93 to $4.2\times10^4$ at 1\,atm), and with
it, in the overall coefficient, the film that controls.  In the curved-line
panel slide the gas coefficient down: the interface walks along the curve, and
the slope choices that agreed to a percent drift apart.  In the reaction panel
press Missen's two cases and watch the point move from the region where $E$
bends towards $E_i$ to the diagonal where $\mathrm{Ha}/\tanh\mathrm{Ha}$ is enough.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a Henry's-law constant is ``large'' or ``small''.}  Without its
type (solubility or volatility) and its composition variables, the number says
nothing; Sander's compilation opens on this warning.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

One solute through one film (no multicomponent matrices); low-flux coefficients
corrected only for the stagnant film (no general finite-flux factor); Henry's
law in the dilute, physical form (no dissociation in water); the film model of a
reacting liquid, irreversible and isothermal (no penetration or surface-renewal
$E$, which the filed sources do not print for a reacting film, no reversible
reaction, no heat of reaction at the interface).

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{The rate-based packed segment}
(\theorydest{sec:rateBasedSegment}): the engine's own film equations, the
bootstrap and the interface energy balance.}

\subsection{Heat exchange: effectiveness against NTU}\label{sec:edu-entu}

\declaredanchor{ch:hx-entu}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have an exchanger of area $A$ and overall coefficient $U$, and two
streams with known inlet temperatures and flows.  What duty do I actually
get --- and if I want more, will more area give it to me?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

The chart is the family of $\varepsilon(\mathrm{NTU})$ curves at several
capacity ratios $C_r$, with the solved exchanger's own point placed on the
curve for its own $C_r$.  A chip reports whether the drawn geometry
reproduces the engine's effectiveness; when it does not, that is a
statement about the configuration the tool assumed, never about the point.

\begin{tryit}
Double the area, from $10$ to $20$~m\textsuperscript{2}, and read the duty
before and after.  Then double it again, to $40$.  Note how much of the
first doubling you got back on the second.  Now set the two heat-capacity
rates far apart (raise one flow, drop the other), so $C_r \to 0$, and
switch between counter-current and co-current: the two curves close up
until they lie on top of each other.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Area $A$ and coefficient $U$} --- both enter only through
        $\mathrm{NTU} = UA/C_{\min}$, which is the first thing to notice:
        a better coefficient and a bigger exchanger are the same move on
        this chart.
  \item \textbf{The two flows} --- they set $C_{\min}$, $C_{\max}$ and
        hence both $\mathrm{NTU}$ and $C_r$, so a flow change slides the
        point along one curve \emph{and} moves it to another.
  \item \textbf{The two inlet temperatures} --- they do not move the point
        at all.  They set $Q_{\max}$, and therefore the duty in kilowatts,
        but $\varepsilon$, $\mathrm{NTU}$ and $C_r$ are all independent of
        them.  Verifying that is a good five minutes.
  \item \textbf{The configuration} --- counter-current, co-current, or one
        shell with two tube passes.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That duty scales with area.}  $\mathrm{NTU}$ is linear in $A$;
$\varepsilon$ is emphatically not.  Every curve on the chart bends over,
and past roughly $\mathrm{NTU} = 3$ the return on further area is nearly
nothing --- the exchanger is already delivering most of what the inlet
temperatures permit, and no amount of metal buys what thermodynamics has
capped.  Students who have only seen $Q = UA\,\Delta T_{LM}$ read a
proportionality that is not there, because $\Delta T_{LM}$ collapses as
the exchanger gets good.
\end{pitfall}

The second one is more interesting, and the chart proves it in one move:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{That counter-current is always better.}  It is never
        worse, but at $C_r = 0$ --- one stream condensing or boiling, or
        simply so large that its temperature barely moves --- the two
        arrangements give \emph{identical} effectiveness, and the curves
        coincide exactly.  The advantage of counter-current flow is
        purchased entirely by the temperature change of the smaller
        stream, so when that change is absent there is nothing to buy.
        This is worth doing on the screen because it is the kind of fact
        that is remembered as a slogan and applied where it does not hold.
  \item \textbf{That $\varepsilon$ is an efficiency, so $\varepsilon =
        0.75$ means a quarter is wasted.}  Nothing is wasted;
        the exchanger is adiabatic to its surroundings and every joule
        that leaves the hot stream arrives in the cold one.  $\varepsilon$
        is normalised by $Q_{\max} = C_{\min}(T_{h,\mathrm{in}} -
        T_{c,\mathrm{in}})$, a ceiling set by the \emph{inlet
        temperatures}, which is a statement about what an infinitely
        large exchanger could do --- not about losses.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{It is a rating result, not a design.}  ``Given this
        hardware, what happens?'' is the question answered.  ``What
        hardware do I need?'' is a different loop, with pressure drop and
        geometry in it.
  \item \textbf{No pressure drop, no fouling, no cost.}  The chart's flat
        tail says extra area stops paying in duty; it says nothing about
        what the area costs, and nothing about the pumping power the extra
        length consumes.
  \item \textbf{Heat-capacity rates are taken as constant} across the
        pass.  A stream whose $c_p$ swings, or which changes phase part
        way, is outside the assumption.
  \item \textbf{Three configurations only.}  Cross-flow arrangements, and
        the correction factors that go with them, are not offered here.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Two-stream heat exchanger: the
$\varepsilon$--NTU rating method} (\theorydest{ch:hx-entu}) --- the
definitions of $C_r$ and $\mathrm{NTU}$, the counter-current and
co-current closed forms, the duty from $Q_{\max}$, and the
$UA\,\mathrm{LMTD} = Q$ self-check.  The design problem the rating chart
does not answer --- geometry, pressure drop, the sizing loop --- is the
section immediately following it, \emph{Heat exchanger DESIGN}.}

\subsection{Heat integration: the composite curves and the pinch}\label{sec:edu-pinch}

\declaredanchor{ch:pinch}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{Before a single exchanger is drawn: what is the least hot utility
and the least cold utility this set of streams can possibly need?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

The tool draws what the engine's pinch pass published and computes
nothing itself: the stream table (every segment's $T_{\mathrm{in}}$,
$T_{\mathrm{out}}$, $|Q|$ and $CP = |Q|/|\Delta T|$), the worked
Linnhoff--Flower cascade row by row, the composite curves (the cold curve
slid by $Q_{C,\min}$, with the two utility intervals, the maximum recovery
and the $\Delta T_{\min}$ gap at the pinch marked), the grand composite and
the candidate table, a feasibility assessment of each hot--cold pair whose
$Q_{\max}$ values are limits of single pairs and are not additive.  The
engine also \emph{classifies} the problem from the whole cascade: an
interior pinch, several pinches, or a threshold problem with no hot or no
cold utility, in which case there is no pinch and none is drawn.  The
net $Q_{H,\min}-Q_{C,\min}$ is the same at every $\Delta T_{\min}$ (it is
the cold total minus the hot total), which is a check a student can make
on paper.

\begin{tryit}
Change nothing but $\Delta T_{\min}$ --- from $20$~K down to $10$, then up
to $30$.  Watch the composite curves slide together and apart, the two
targets move in opposite directions, and the pinch temperature itself
change.  Nothing physical has been altered: not a stream, not a flow, not
a temperature.  Ask the class what, then, has changed.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{$\Delta T_{\min}$} --- the decisive knob, and the one that
        is a \emph{choice}.
  \item \textbf{The supply temperatures} --- move one stream's start and
        watch whether the pinch stays where it was.  Sometimes it does
        not, and the region boundaries in the candidate table move with it.
  \item \textbf{The flows} --- a flow scales that stream's $CP$, which
        changes the slope of its segment of the composite curve.  A steep
        segment and a flat one are the same duty over different
        temperature spans.
  \item \textbf{The candidate table} --- read the temperature-feasibility
        column and the pinch-rule column separately.  A match may be
        thermodynamically possible and still violate the $CP$ rule at the
        pinch.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the pinch is a property of the plant.}  It is a property of
the stream data \emph{plus a number the engineer chose}.  Turning
$\Delta T_{\min}$ moves the pinch temperature and both targets while the
process stands still.  Students routinely quote ``the pinch is at
$115^{\circ}$C'' as though it had been measured; it was assumed into
existence, half of it by the choice of driving force, and the value of
that choice is an economic trade-off between exchanger area and utility
bill that this pass does not make for you.
\end{pitfall}

Two more, and the third is the one that decides coursework marks:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{That a target is a design.}  The table lists
        \emph{thermodynamically admissible candidates} and never ranks
        them.  The word ``optimal'' does not appear anywhere in this pass,
        and a gate in the test suite enforces that.  Reaching the target
        may require more exchangers, and more awkwardly placed ones, than
        anybody would build; plot plan, control, start-up and safety are
        not in the temperature table.
  \item \textbf{That heating below the pinch merely costs a little
        extra.}  It costs \emph{exactly its own duty}, and so does cooling
        above the pinch, and the two are additive:
        \[
          \sum(\text{heating below}) + \sum(\text{cooling above})
          \;=\; Q_{H,\mathrm{current}} - Q_{H,\min}.
        \]
        The violation diagnostics report both sums, and they are computed
        by different code from the targets --- so the identity closing is
        a check you can do by hand and the tool can fail.  A student who
        has only the slogan ``do not transfer heat across the pinch''
        cannot say how much a violation costs; this identity says exactly
        how much.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Energy targets only.}  Area targets, capital targets, and
        the capital-versus-energy trade-off that would let you
        \emph{choose} $\Delta T_{\min}$ economically are a further phase
        and are not implemented.  The Theory Guide states this in the same
        words rather than describing something the engine does not have.
  \item \textbf{It never rewrites the network.}  The flowsheet you wrote
        is the flowsheet that ran; the pass analyses it and stops.
  \item \textbf{A unit enters as its net duty.}  A column with a reboiler
        and a condenser is a single net number unless you model the two as
        separate units --- and for a column that is usually the wrong
        picture.  Splitting is the fix, and it is your decision, not the
        pass's.
  \item \textbf{The grand composite curve is derived in view.}  The engine
        publishes the hot, cold and shifted composite curves; the tool
        builds the grand composite from those, and labels it as such.  The
        cross-check chip exists so you can see that the derivation
        reproduces the engine's targets.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Pinch analysis: the target before the
network} (\theorydest{ch:pinch}) --- the $CP$ line, why $\Delta T_{\min}$
shifts the axes, the problem table cascade by cascade, the composite
curves, the candidate matches, the violation identity, and an explicit
\emph{What this pass does not do}.}

\subsection{Reactor sizing: the Levenspiel plot}\label{sec:edu-levenspiel}

\declaredanchor{ch:pfr}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{The same reaction, the same feed, the same conversion --- which
reactor is smaller, a plug-flow tube or a stirred tank, and by how much?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

One chart carries $1/(-r_A)$ against conversion $X_A$, and on it two
areas: the plug-flow reactor's integral $\int_0^{X_{\mathrm{out}}}
\mathrm{d}X/(-r_A)$ under the curve, and the stirred tank's rectangle of
height $1/(-r_A)$ \emph{at the outlet} and width $X_{\mathrm{out}}$.  Both
witnesses are twins --- byte-identical feed, kinetics and thermodynamics
--- so the comparison is between reactors and never between chemistries.

\begin{tryit}
Raise the plug-flow reactor's volume so $X_{\mathrm{out}}$ climbs, and
watch the rectangle and the area separate.  At low conversion they nearly
coincide; at high conversion the rectangle towers over the area.  Then
lower the temperature: the whole curve lifts (a slower rate is a larger
ordinate) and both reactors grow --- but the \emph{ratio} between them is
governed by the curve's shape, not its height.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Reactor temperature} --- through the Arrhenius constant.
  \item \textbf{The two feed flows} --- the limiting reactant sets
        $F_{A0}$ and the co-reactant changes what the rate law sees.
  \item \textbf{The plug-flow volume} --- the knob that walks
        $X_{\mathrm{out}}$ along the curve, which is the axis the whole
        lesson lives on.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the stirred tank needs more volume because it is somehow a
worse reactor.}  Students reach for a hardware explanation --- poorer
mixing, dead zones, short-circuiting.  The chart shows that the cause is
the opposite and is purely geometric: a stirred tank is \emph{perfectly}
mixed, so every molecule in it sits at the outlet composition and
therefore at the outlet rate --- the \emph{lowest} rate anywhere in the
duty.  The tube sees the whole range of rates from inlet to outlet.  The
rectangle circumscribes the area because perfect mixing throws away every
fast rate the reaction was willing to give.  \emph{Being well mixed is
what costs the volume.}
\end{pitfall}

And immediately after that, the guard against over-learning it:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{That ``a CSTR always needs more volume'' is a law.}  It
        follows from the curve \emph{rising} with conversion, which is
        what positive-order kinetics do.  Draw a rate law whose
        $1/(-r_A)$ has a minimum --- autocatalysis, strong product
        inhibition --- and the rectangle can be the smaller of the two
        over part of the range, which is why real plants sometimes put a
        tank first and a tube after it.  The construction is valuable
        exactly because it is a picture and not a rule: it shows you how
        to check, on any kinetics, rather than what to remember.
  \item \textbf{That the smaller reactor is the cheaper one.}  Volume is
        not price.  A large stirred tank can be considerably cheaper than
        a small tubular reactor with the same duty once you count
        fabrication, catalyst containment, cleaning and control.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{The comparison is isothermal.}  Both witnesses run at the
        same declared temperature.  Nothing here shows the thermal
        behaviour that makes the two reactors genuinely different animals
        --- for that, go to \S\ref{sec:edu-vanheerden}.
  \item \textbf{No residence-time distribution.}  Plug flow and perfect
        mixing are the two ideal limits; every real reactor is between
        them, and neither line on this chart is a real vessel.
  \item \textbf{Reversible kinetics are clipped, and the clipping is
        announced.}  Past equilibrium the engine's rate goes negative and
        those points are excluded from the area, with the exclusion stated
        in the chart annotation.  An integral run through them would be
        arithmetic, not physics.
  \item \textbf{The stirred tank's volume is not a knob here}, for a
        recorded reason: the witness declares it inline, out of reach of
        the override grammar the classroom knobs use.  It always runs its
        authored value.  This is stated in the tool's own provenance line
        rather than left to be discovered.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Plug-flow reactor} (\theorydest{ch:pfr})
--- the differential balance, the RK4 integration, and the first-order
analytic check that gives the canonical comparison at $k\tau = 1$
($X^{\mathrm{PFR}} = 1 - e^{-1} = 0.632$ against $X^{\mathrm{CSTR}} =
1/(1+k\tau) = 0.500$), which is this construction in closed form for one
special case.  The stirred tank's own extent formulation is in
\emph{Continuous stirred-tank reactor} (\theorydest{ch:cstr}).}

\subsection{Multiple steady states: the Van~Heerden diagram}\label{sec:edu-vanheerden}

\declaredanchor{ch:cstr}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{The same reactor, the same feed, the same everything --- so why does
it sometimes sit cold and sometimes run hot, and could it be made to sit
in between?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

The diagram is heat \textbf{generated} $G(T)$ against heat
\textbf{removed} $R(T)$, both drawn from the engine's own scan of the
reactor's steady-state equation.  Generation is a sigmoid: the rate rises
exponentially with temperature and then saturates once the reactant is
gone.  Removal is a straight line, because in an adiabatic vessel the only
sink is the sensible heat that leaves with the product.  A straight line
can cut a sigmoid three times.

\begin{tryit}
Drag \code{T\_guess} slowly from $280$~K to $430$~K and watch the
\emph{reported} marker.  For a long stretch nothing happens; then it jumps
to another crossing on a diagram that has not moved at all.  Same
equipment, same feed, same equations --- a different answer, decided by
where you started.  That is not a numerical artefact; it is why real
reactors have a start-up procedure.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{\code{T\_guess}} --- the decisive knob.  The engine finds
        \emph{all} the roots, prints them, and reports the one nearest
        your guess.
  \item \textbf{Feed temperature} --- slides the removal line.  Push it up
        far enough and the two lower crossings merge and vanish; the
        reactor can then only be ignited.
  \item \textbf{Reactor volume} --- stretches the generation sigmoid
        against a removal line that does not move with it.
  \item \textbf{The \code{steadyStates} count and the ignition span} ---
        how many crossings there are, and how far the reactor jumps
        between the coldest and the hottest.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That three answers means the solver is broken.}  Students trained
on ``the equation has \emph{an} answer'' read multiplicity as a defect ---
a bad initial guess, a tolerance too loose, a bug to be reported.  It is
the physics of the system, and the diagram is the proof: an exponential
generation curve and a linear removal line intersect three times over a
range of conditions, and each intersection is a genuine steady state of
the same hardware.  The initial guess is not a numerical convenience in
this problem.  It is the start-up procedure, written as a number.
\end{pitfall}

The one that is harder, and better:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{That the middle state is merely difficult to converge
        to.}  It is not difficult; it is \emph{unstable}.  A steady-state
        solver will sit on it happily, which is exactly what makes it
        dangerous pedagogy: the number exists and the reactor cannot hold
        it.  Nudge the temperature up and generation exceeds removal, so
        it climbs further; nudge it down and it falls to the extinguished
        state.  ``The solver converged'' and ``the plant can run there''
        are different claims, and this is the cleanest demonstration in
        the corpus that the second does not follow from the first.
  \item \textbf{That the ignited state is the one you want.}  It is the
        one with the conversion.  It may also be past a selectivity
        limit, past a catalyst's sintering temperature, or past the
        vessel's design pressure --- none of which appear on a diagram
        whose only axes are temperature and heat.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{It is a steady-state diagram.}  It says which states
        \emph{exist}.  It does not say how the reactor travels between
        them, how long ignition takes, whether it overshoots, or what the
        transient does to the catalyst.  That is the dynamic simulator's
        subject.
  \item \textbf{The instability flag is an ordering, not an analysis.}
        With exactly three roots the tool marks the middle one, which is
        arithmetic on the engine's own root list.  With any other count it
        makes \emph{no} stability claim at all --- a restraint worth
        pointing out to a class, because it is the difference between a
        result and a habit.
  \item \textbf{The removal line is straight because this reactor is
        adiabatic.}  Add a cooling jacket at a fixed coolant temperature
        and it is still straight, with a different slope and intercept;
        let the coolant temperature vary along the vessel and it is not
        straight at all, and the geometry of ``how many crossings'' has to
        be re-argued.
  \item \textbf{The kinetics are a declared teaching variant.}  The
        witness's own header says so, and its Arrhenius parameters are
        deliberately \emph{not} knobs: a construction about start-up
        should not silently become a construction about a different
        chemistry.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Continuous stirred-tank reactor}
(\theorydest{ch:cstr}) --- the material balance in the reaction extent and
the one-dimensional solve the engine performs, which is the equation whose
roots this diagram displays.  The witness case
\file{tutorials/steady/reactors/cstr05\_multiplicity} carries the
generation-versus-removal argument in its own \file{flowsheetDict} header,
and is the fullest written statement of the construction in the tree; the
tool quotes it.  (That witness no longer exhibits three roots: after the
acetic-acid correction its liquid esterification is nearly thermoneutral and
the scan finds one; a replacement exothermic witness is planned.)}

\section{The correlation library, and how to read a disagreement}
\label{sec:edu-correlations}

Every construction in \S\ref{sec:edu-tools} rests on correlations, and a
simulator normally hides them.  It picks one, computes with it, and prints
a number.  The student learns that the friction factor \emph{is} $0.0223$,
in the same tone of voice the simulator uses for the molar mass of water.

It is not.  It is one correlation's answer, and which one was chosen is
part of the answer.

This section is about the instruments that make that visible.  They are
not charts --- they run under \code{choupoProps} and print --- but they
teach something no chart can, because the thing to be seen is a
\emph{disagreement between authorities}, and a chart of one curve cannot
show it.

\subsection{What a correlation carries}

A correlation in Choupo is an object, and it is required to answer four
questions about itself:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{What am I?} --- the expression, evaluated.
  \item \textbf{Where do I stop being valid?} --- the window its own
        authors stated, in words, printed on every run.
  \item \textbf{Who says so?} --- a dated primary citation.  A correlation
        whose source lives in a code comment is one the reader cannot
        check.
  \item \textbf{Can I still prove it?} --- a \emph{verify} against a
        published anchor, re-run every time, with the deviation printed
        beside the source.
\end{itemize}

The fourth is the unusual one.  A self-check that nobody wires up is a
promise, not a check; running it on every use is what turns it into one.

\subsection{The friction factor: four answers to one question}
\label{sec:edu-friction}

\declaredanchor{ch:hydraulics-pressure}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{Water at $\mathrm{Re} = 10^{5}$ in a commercial steel line,
$\varepsilon/D = 10^{-3}$.  What is the Darcy friction factor?}

Run \code{tutorials/props/hydraulics/moody01\_friction\_correlations} and
four correlations answer:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
correlation & $f$ & \\
\midrule
Blasius     & $0.017770$ & \textbf{outside its window} \\
Churchill   & $0.022343$ & \\
Colebrook   & $0.022175$ & \\
Haaland     & $0.021966$ & \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{spread over all four} & $25.74\,\%$ \\
\multicolumn{2}{l}{spread over the three inside their windows} & $1.72\,\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection*{The two numbers, and why there are two}

Read the last two rows together, because either one alone is misleading.

\emph{The correlations disagree by 26\,\%} is what the first row says, and
it is false.  Three of them agree within $1.7\,\%$ --- which for a
correlated quantity is agreement, not disagreement.

What produces the $25.74\,\%$ is Blasius, and Blasius is not wrong.  It is
being \emph{misused}.  Blasius carries no roughness term at all: it was
fitted to smooth pipes and knows nothing about a wall.  Asked about
$\varepsilon/D = 10^{-3}$ it answers anyway, confidently, twenty per cent
low, and there is nothing about the number $0.017770$ that looks wrong.

That is the whole content of a validity window, and it is why this run
prints one for every correlation on every call.  The window is not
bureaucracy attached to the formula.  It is the only thing standing
between a plausible number and a reader.

\begin{tryit}
Set \code{relRough} to $0$ --- a hydraulically smooth pipe --- and run
again.  Blasius comes back inside its window, the two spreads collapse
onto each other, and the four answers agree to a few per cent.  Now raise
\code{relRough} to $10^{-2}$ and watch the gap between the two spreads
widen: the more the wall matters, the more expensive Blasius's silence
about it becomes.

Then set \code{Re} to $1500$.  Three of the four announce that they have
substituted the exact laminar law $f = 64/\mathrm{Re}$, because they are
turbulent fits and say so.  Churchill does not need to: its single
expression contains the laminar law as its own limit, which is why it is
the default and why its self-check is anchored there.
\end{tryit}

\subsubsection*{What the anchors actually test --- and it differs}

The run prints a deviation for each correlation against a published
anchor.  It would be easy to read four small numbers as four validations.
They are not the same kind of statement, and the output says which is
which:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Blasius} is checked against its own closed form.  This
        tests the \emph{arithmetic} and nothing else --- and the run says
        so, so that a deviation of $0.000\,\%$ cannot be mistaken for
        agreement with a measurement.
  \item \textbf{Colebrook} is checked in the fully-rough limit, where its
        implicit equation collapses to a closed form.  This tests that the
        fixed-point iteration \emph{arrives}.
  \item \textbf{Haaland} is checked against \emph{Colebrook}, because
        agreement within about $2\,\%$ is precisely the claim Haaland
        published.  Anchoring him to anything else would be checking a
        claim nobody made.
  \item \textbf{Churchill} is checked against the exact laminar law.  This
        is the strongest of the four: it is a property the expression was
        built to have and that no coding error preserves by accident.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\textbf{None of these four is checked against experimental data.}  Choupo
carries no friction measurements at all.  Two of the anchors are
self-consistency by construction and one is agreement with another
correlation; only Colebrook's tests an algorithm against a closed form the
equation itself provides.  The run passes, and what it establishes is that
the code computes what the papers wrote --- not that the papers are right.

The bench also never says which correlation to use.  That depends on the
pipe: its roughness, whether the flow is developed, whether the duct is
even circular.  A tool that ranked them would be making the engineer's
decision quietly, which is exactly what a silent default does.

And nothing here speaks about the transition band,
$2300 < \mathrm{Re} < 4000$, where no correlation was regressed and the
flow itself is not single-valued.  Correlations evaluated there are marked
outside their window, and that mark is the entire answer available.

\subsection{One family so far}

This discipline covers the convective heat-transfer correlations
(\code{heatTransferBench}) and, since 2026-08-25, the friction factor
(\code{frictionBench}).  It does \emph{not} yet cover the rest: the engine
computes Wilke--Chang, Chilton--Colburn, Thiele, Weisz, Joback,
Lee--Kesler, Rackett and Watson, and those remain internal to the
operations that use them --- correct, cited in comments, and invisible to a
student.

Saying so is the point.  A guide that implied an exhaustive catalogue
where two families exist would be committing the error this whole section
is about: presenting one answer as though it were the only one.

\section{One number for ``like dissolves like''}
\label{sec:edu-solubility}

A student asked to pick a solvent has a shelf of bottles and no way to
rank them.  The oldest useful answer is a single number per liquid, and
it is worth teaching precisely because it is \emph{almost} right --- the
places where it fails are as instructive as the places where it works.

\subsection{Where the number comes from}

Vaporising a liquid pulls its molecules apart.  The energy that costs,
per unit volume, is the \emph{cohesive energy density}; its square root
is Hildebrand's solubility parameter:

\begin{equation}
  \delta \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{vap}}(T) - RT}{V_m}}
  \label{eq:hildebrand}
\end{equation}

Two things in that expression are worth stopping on.

The $RT$ is the work the vapour does pushing the atmosphere aside.
Subtracting it turns an \emph{enthalpy} of vaporisation into an
\emph{energy} of vaporisation, and only the second one measures cohesion.
Leaving it in gives a $\delta$ a few per cent high --- small enough to
look right, which is what makes it a favourite error.

And $\delta$ is a function of temperature that nobody writes the
temperature next to.  As a liquid approaches its critical point the
cohesive energy goes to zero, and so does $\delta$: there is no longer a
liquid to pull apart.  Published tables are at $25\,^\circ$C; a
$\delta$ quoted without a temperature is a number from an unstated state.

\subsection{Running it}

\begin{center}
\code{choupoProps} with a \code{solubilityParameter} operation.
\end{center}

Choupo \emph{derives} $\delta$ --- it is never stored.  Every input is
already in the component record (the latent heat at $T_b$, the critical
temperature, the liquid molar volume), so a stored $\delta$ would be a
second home for a fact the record already fixes, free to drift from it.
The latent heat at $T$ comes from the same Watson correlation the
enthalpy legs use, not a second copy of it.

The output has three parts: what each substance's $\delta$ is and what it
was computed from; how far that sits from an independently published
value where the record carries one; and the pairwise table
$|\delta_i - \delta_j|$, which is what the question actually was.

\subsection{The column that makes it checkable}

\begin{tryit}
Look at the \emph{published} column before you look at anything else.

Equation~\ref{eq:hildebrand} has three chances to go quietly wrong: a unit
slip in $V_m$, the missing $RT$, and reporting $\mathrm{Pa}^{0.5}$ where
the literature uses $\mathrm{MPa}^{0.5}$ --- a factor of $31.6$ that still
looks like a solubility parameter.  None of the three raises an error.

What catches them is that some records carry a $\delta$ somebody else
computed, independently, and the run prints the deviation.  Across the
substances where both exist the derivation reproduces the published value
to well under a per cent; a deviation of several per cent is therefore a
\emph{finding} and not noise.

This is the same device as the $Z_c$ column in a property data bank: a
redundant determination turns a plausible wrong number into a visible
disagreement.  A tool that computes something you cannot check is a tool
that is right until the day it is not.
\end{tryit}

\subsection{Where one number stops}

\textbf{Hildebrand cannot separate the hydrogen bonding it contains.}
The cohesive energy it measures \emph{includes} the hydrogen bonds broken
on boiling; what one number cannot do is tell that part from dispersion
and polarity.  Ethanol ($\delta \approx 26~\mathrm{MPa}^{1/2}$) and
nitromethane ($\approx 25.1$) sit almost on top of each other, and a
formulator knows they behave differently --- one donates hydrogen bonds
and the other does not.  The theory cannot express the difference,
because it compressed every kind of intermolecular force into one scalar.

That is why the coatings industry uses Hansen's three-way split:
dispersion, polar and hydrogen-bonding contributions, with miscibility
judged by a distance in that space rather than on a line.  Choupo reads
the split from a \code{hansen \{\}} block on a record and measures that
distance with the \code{hansenMixture} operation --- the EduTool
\emph{Choosing a solvent, or a solvent mixture} draws it; the catalogue
carries no split itself, and the witnesses carry theirs case-locally
with a citation per value.

So the honest reading of a close pair is \emph{candidate}, never
\emph{verdict} --- and the run prints exactly that sentence, on every
call, rather than letting a small number imply more than it carries.

\begin{tryit}
Put water in the list.  Its $\delta$ is enormous
($\approx 48~\mathrm{MPa}^{1/2}$), far from every organic on
the shelf, and the pairwise table will duly report that water mixes with
nothing.  Water and ethanol are of course miscible in all proportions.

That single row is the clearest lesson the tool gives: the number is a
measure of \emph{how much} energy holds a liquid together, and says
nothing about \emph{what kind}.
\end{tryit}

\subsection{Adsorption isotherms from laboratory data}\label{sec:edu-adsorption-isotherms}

\declaredanchor{sec:adsorption-isotherm-fitting}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=adsorption-isotherms}.
\textit{\small Case it starts from:} none --- the page fits points it
generates or points you paste, and runs no engine.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have laboratory equilibrium points $(c, q)$ for a solute on an
adsorbent; which isotherm describes them, with what parameters, and how far
can I trust it?}

The page opens on what an isotherm is --- the loading on the solid against
the concentration left in the liquid, at one temperature, each point read
from a flask balance $q = V(c_0-c)/M$ --- and on the three shapes
(favourable, linear, unfavourable) and what each does to a front in a bed,
which is where the \code{breakthrough} page continues.  It then derives the
three models: linear $q = Kc$; Langmuir $q = q_{\max}c/(K_L+c)$ from a site
balance, with its equivalent affinity form $b = 1/K_L$ and its Henry limit
$q_{\max}/K_L$; and Freundlich $q = K_F c^{n}$ (the exponent written as the course text writes it; Seader writes it $1/n$), which has neither a Henry
limit nor a plateau.

\subsubsection*{What the page computes, and what it does not}

Everything is the page's own arithmetic, marked \emph{page} on every panel.
The engine carries Henry and Langmuir isotherms for a gas, in partial
pressure, and fits them to measured $(T, p, q)$ data; it has no
liquid-phase isotherm and no Freundlich model, and handing it a
concentration as a pressure would be a units lie.  The page fits each model
two ways: the classical straight lines ($1/q$ against $1/c$; $\log q$
against $\log c$) and a direct least-squares fit on $q$ by a hand-written
Levenberg--Marquardt, with absolute or relative residuals.  It prints each
parameter with its standard error from $s^2(J^{\mathsf T}WJ)^{-1}$, the
root-mean-square residual, the largest relative error, a residual plot,
and a diagnostic of whether the measured range can tell the models apart:
the decades of $c$ spanned, the fraction of the fitted Langmuir plateau
reached at the highest and the lowest point, and how many standard errors
the Freundlich exponent lies from 1.  Every plot shades the concentrations
outside the measured range.  Finally the fitted isotherm is used once: the
single-stage batch balance $Vc_0 + Mq_0 = Vc + Mq$ drawn as an operating
line, giving the dose for a target removal, with a warning when the target
lies outside the data.  The design record
\file{docs/design/adsorption-isotherms-from-laboratory-data.md} holds the
measurements and a proposal for the engine side.

\subsubsection*{The examples are synthetic}

Three example sets are \emph{generated} by the page, not measured, and are
labelled so: each states its model, its parameters, its concentrations and
its noise (multiplicative, $\exp(\sigma e)$ with a seeded standard normal
$e$), so the fit can be checked against the truth.  An organic solute on
activated carbon, Freundlich-shaped over four decades of $c$; a metal ion on
a chelating resin, strongly favourable Langmuir with a clear plateau; and a
trace solute generated from a Langmuir isotherm but measured only far below
$K_L$, where nothing in the data can tell the three models apart.  Your own
points are pasted as two columns, in a unit chosen from a short list.

\begin{tryit}
On the activated-carbon set, compare the Langmuir straight-line fit with the
direct one: the double-reciprocal line gives a capacity about four times
smaller (13.5 against 58.5 mg/g), because the lowest concentrations dominate
it, and neither Langmuir fit describes a set that has no plateau --- the
Freundlich RMS is about a fifth of the direct Langmuir's.  On the trace-solute set the
double-reciprocal line has $R^2 = 0.999$ and still returns $q_{\max} = 124$
mg/g where the direct fit gives $64 \pm 29$: a high $R^2$ on the transformed
variables says the points lie near a line, not that the capacity is known.
Read that standard error and the diagnostic sentence, then switch the
residuals from absolute to relative and watch the Freundlich exponent move
towards or away from 1.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{Reading the smallest RMS as the right model.}  A two-parameter
model always fits at least as well as a one-parameter one.  The residual
plot --- a run of one sign then the other --- and the diagnostic of the
measured range say more than the RMS alone.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

One temperature: no heat of adsorption, no van't Hoff extrapolation.  One
solute: no competition.  Equilibrium only: no uptake rate.  One batch
stage: no multistage dosing and no column.  The standard errors rest on the
chosen error model and on local linearity.

\subsection{Linear algebra without the pain}\label{sec:edu-linear-algebra-without-pain}

\declaredanchor{ch:linear-algebra-without-pain}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=linear-algebra-without-pain}.
\textit{\small Case it starts from:} none --- the page is arithmetic on toy
matrices and runs no engine; what is the engine's is each file and line it

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{Why is a simulator full of matrices, and what are LU, pivoting and the
conjugate gradient for?}

\subsubsection*{What the page shows}

Eight steps in lay language, each a sketch first, then the symbol, then where
Choupo does it, with a small figure on $2\times2$ or $3\times3$ numbers you

\emph{definite}, \emph{semi-definite} and \emph{indefinite} with what the

\begin{tryit}

\end{tryit}

or Freundlich isotherm you type --- for instance the one fitted on the
\code{adsorption-isotherms} page.  One basis throughout: $c$ in mg/L, $q$ in
mg/g, $V$ in L, $M$ in g.

\subsubsection*{What the page computes, and what it does not}

Everything is the page's own arithmetic, marked \emph{page}.  The engine's
batch adsorber and fixed bed contact a gas, in partial pressure, with Henry or
Langmuir isotherms; it has no liquid-phase adsorption and no Freundlich model.
The isotherm functions are the \code{adsorption-isotherms} page's, imported,
not copied.  Step by step: the equilibrium of one stage as the crossing of the
operating line (slope $-V/M$) with the isotherm, found by bisection on a
bracket that always holds and printed beside the closed form where one exists
(linear, Langmuir); the dose for a target removal, drawn against the removal on
a logarithmic axis, with a pre-loaded adsorbent's equilibrium floor refused by
name; one to four stages in crossflow and in countercurrent, each with its
mass balance recomputed, and the best split of a fixed total between two
crossflow stages; the uptake in time with the whole resistance in a liquid
film, $k_L$ from $\mathit{Sh} = 2$ (the still-liquid bound, which a stirred tank
exceeds by one to two orders of magnitude, so the times printed by default are an
upper bound; the student may replace it), $a$ from the particle density, in closed form for a linear isotherm
and integrated numerically (fourth-order Runge--Kutta) otherwise, with the time
to a target at five particle sizes; and the Biot number with Glueckauf's
series resistance, with a warning when diffusion inside the particle makes the
film-only times too short.  The design record
\file{docs/design/batch-adsorption-in-a-stirred-tank.md} holds the
measurements and a proposal for the engine side.

\begin{tryit}
On the dye example with 50~g in 100~L, read the best two-stage split: about
69\,\% of the carbon in the first stage, leaving 0.92~mg/L against 1.15~mg/L for
half and half.  Switch to the linear example and watch the optimum return to
exactly one half.  Then, in the kinetic step, halve the particle diameter and
check that every time falls by four --- the still-liquid result, which holds
because $\mathit{Sh}$ is fixed --- and then declare $\mathit{Sh}=100$ to see the
times a stirred tank gives.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{Reading $\mathit{Sh}=2$ as a stirred tank.}  It is a sphere in still
liquid, and a stirred tank's Sherwood number is tens to a few hundred for
particles of this size (Treybal's Eq.~11.29 gives about 190 for 0.5~mm at
1~kW/m$^3$; Seader's Example~15.10 has $k_L = 5\times10^{-5}$~m/s), so the
default times are one to two orders of magnitude too long.  And the Biot number
does not fall as the particle shrinks \emph{while $\mathit{Sh}$ is fixed}: at
$\mathit{Sh} = 2$ it is $D_{AB}/D_e$, larger than one for every porous particle;
in a tank $\mathit{Sh}$ grows with the diameter and so does $\mathit{Bi}$.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

One temperature and no heat of adsorption; one solute; a perfectly mixed
tank; no diffusion solved inside the particle (only flagged); no
continuous column, which is the \code{breakthrough} page.

\subsection{Adsorption: sizing a bed from a breakthrough curve (LUB)}\label{sec:edu-lub-scaleup}

\declaredanchor{ch:adsorption}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=lub-scaleup}.  \textit{\small
Witness:} \file{tutorials/batch/adsorber/batch13\_breakthrough\_co2}, the
same laboratory column the \code{breakthrough} tool integrates.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have a breakthrough curve from a short laboratory column: outlet
over feed concentration against time, at a known length, velocity, feed
and packing.  The plant needs a bed that holds a given feed flow for a
given service time before the outlet goes off specification.  How long,
what diameter, and how much adsorbent?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

The construction is the length-of-unused-bed method, performed on the
screen in the order a student performs it on paper, with the engine's own
numbers printed beside each step.  Two times are read off the curve: the
breakthrough time $t_b$ at the declared criterion, and the stoichiometric
time $t_{st}$ as the trapezoid area above the curve.  The unused length
$\mathrm{LUB} = L_{\mathrm{lab}}(1 - t_b/t_{st})$ is carried to full scale
unchanged, the equilibrium section is the laboratory column scaled by
$t_{\mathrm{req}}/t_{st}$, and the diameter follows from the design flow
at the \emph{same} superficial velocity.  Two families of knobs sit beside
the plot, and the page says which is which: the laboratory column's
velocity, length, temperature, pressure and horizon re-run the engine in
the browser; the criterion, the service time and the design flow are
post-processing and re-draw only, because none of them changes the curve
the column produced.

\begin{tryit}
Start from the witness.  Read the first table: the trapezoid $t_{st}$ and
the engine's own analytic $t_{st}$ (its pre-run claim
$(L/u)\,R_f$) agree to about four digits (the tool integrates the trajectory samples, 100~s apart), and the tool says the gap is
the mesh plus the dropped tail.  Now shorten the horizon knob to
$3200$~s: the outlet has not reached the feed value, the page flags the
data as \emph{incomplete}, and every length in the last table turns into
a lower bound.  Put the horizon back.  Move the criterion from $0.05$ to
$0.02$ and watch $t_b$ slide left while $t_{st}$ does not move: the
unused length grows, because a stricter specification switches earlier.
Finally raise $t_{\mathrm{req}}$ from $10\,000$~s to $40\,000$~s and
watch $\eta_{\mathrm{bed}}$ climb toward one --- the same few centimetres
of unused bed, added once to a bed four times longer.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Breakthrough criterion} --- a specification, not a property
        of the bed.  It moves $t_b$ and therefore LUB; it never moves
        $t_{st}$.
  \item \textbf{Required service time $t_{\mathrm{req}}$} --- the knob that
        sets the equilibrium length, linearly.  Watch the utilisation
        $\eta_{\mathrm{bed}} = L_{es}/L_{\mathrm{full}}$ rise with it.
  \item \textbf{Design feed flow $Q$} --- sets the diameter alone,
        $D = \sqrt{4Q/(\pi u)}$, and through it the adsorbent mass.
  \item \textbf{Bed length $L$} (engine knob) --- both times move, the band
        between them does not: LUB is a property of the conditions, and the
        plot makes that visible.
  \item \textbf{Velocity $u$} (engine knob) --- the one knob that changes
        LUB itself, which is exactly why the scale-up holds $u$ fixed.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the plant bed is the laboratory column scaled by length.}  A
student who has measured a column that wastes five per cent of itself
expects a plant bed to waste five per cent too, and sizes it as
$L_{\mathrm{lab}}\,t_{\mathrm{req}}/t_{st}$ with a five per cent margin.
The unused length is not a fraction; it is a \emph{length}, set by the
width of the mass-transfer zone, and under constant pattern it is the same
length in every bed run at these conditions.  It is added once.  A
half-metre column that throws away $2.5$~cm looks poor; a three-metre bed
with the same $2.5$~cm unused wastes under one per cent.  Laboratory
columns look terrible and plant beds do not, and the page's last table is
that sentence as numbers.
\end{pitfall}

A second confusion, the one that costs a plant:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{That a curve stopped early is a curve.}  The stoichiometric
        time is an \emph{area}, and an area cut before the outlet reached
        the feed value is too small --- so the equilibrium capacity implied
        by the curve falls short of the isotherm's, and every length sized
        from it is short.  The capacity check in the third step exists for
        this: the curve's own balance against the engine's retention
        factor, and a disagreement points at the data, never at the
        isotherm.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What the picture does not say}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Constant pattern is assumed.}  A favourable isotherm and a
        column longer than the zone buy it; a linear or unfavourable
        isotherm, or a column the zone never fits in, gives a curve the
        method sizes from wrongly with no warning on the page.
  \item \textbf{Same velocity, temperature and particle size.}  LUB is
        transferred, never scaled.  A plant bed at other conditions needs a
        curve measured at those conditions.
  \item \textbf{Isothermal, single adsorbate, one loading step.}  The
        witness is declared isothermal, feeds one adsorbing species, and
        starts clean; regeneration and the cycle are other cases' subject
        (\file{batch21\_tsa\_hot\_purge}, \file{batch23\_tsa\_cycles}), named
        on the page and not taught by it.
  \item \textbf{The engine's curve is a model.}  By default the plot is the
        engine's integration of the witness on its default second-order
        van Leer scheme, whose mesh adds about 5\,\% to the front's width
        at 100 cells (first-order upwind, which a case may declare, adds
        about 43\,\% and makes the unused length 2.5~cm instead of
        1.75~cm).  A
        measurement enters through the open case: a
        \file{constant/experimental/breakthrough.csv} with a
        \code{t\_s,c\_over\_c0} header is drawn as markers beside the
        engine's curve and put through the same arithmetic, labelled
        measured.  No tutorial ships one today, and the tool says so rather
        than inventing a curve.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Where the derivation lives}

\derivation{Theory Guide, \emph{Adsorption: isotherms, LDF uptake, and the
fixed bed} (\theorydest{ch:adsorption}):
the two equations the bed integrates, the retention factor
$R_f = \varepsilon + \rho_b q^{*}(c_{in})/c_{in}$ and the stoichiometric
time $t_{st} = (L/u)\,R_f$ the unit announces before it runs, and the
mass-transfer zone whose width the unused length measures.  The trapezoid,
the crossing and the scale-up on this page are the hand method itself,
labelled as such, and judged against those engine numbers.}

\subsection{Chromatography: peaks, plates and resolution}\label{sec:edu-chromatography}

\declaredanchor{sec:chromatography}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=chromatography}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none in the corpus --- the page writes a
\code{choupoBatch} case of its own (one fixed-bed adsorber unit, a synthetic
linear sorbent, a recipe that switches the feed back to carrier) and runs it
in the browser.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I want to separate two solutes on a column: when does each come out,
how wide are the peaks, are they resolved, and what changes when I load
more?}

The bed of the breakthrough page is fed a short injection of two solutes
instead of a step.  The page derives the retention time
$t_R = t_0(1+k')$ with $k' = \rho_b K_c/\varepsilon$, draws the plate model
(the Erlang peak of $N$ stirred plates) beside the Gaussian of the same
mean and variance, builds the van Deemter curve $H = A + B/v + Cv$ from the
bed's own parameters (particle diameter, molecular diffusivity, the
linear-driving-force constant), computes the resolution
$R_s = (t_{R,2}-t_{R,1})/(2(\sigma_1+\sigma_2))$ beside the fundamental
resolution equation, cuts the outlet in time for purity and recovery, and
then loads the column until the isotherm bends.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

The engine runs the linear pulse: its outlet histories and its own
stoichiometric times, Peclet number and mass closure are read off the run,
and a run that does not belong to the current knobs is not drawn.  The page
puts the moments derived in the Theory Guide (elution chromatography, in the adsorption chapter) beside it, with the
dispersion the first-order grid adds (one plate per cell) as a
column of its own --- the page's case declares \code{convectionScheme
upwind;} for exactly that reason, since the engine's default van Leer
scheme adds no such term --- and its own second-order integration of the same bed
equations as a third reading.  The overloaded column --- a competitive
Langmuir isotherm, in concentration units --- is the page's integration
only, on 100 cells, with its mass ledger printed and a button that repeats
it on 200 cells and prints the change.  The design record
\file{docs/design/chromatography-peaks-and-resolution.md} holds the moment
derivation, the native measurement that decided to use the engine for the
linear part, the tests, and a table of every published form the page uses
with its checking status.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
At the defaults the two peaks overlap ($R_s$ just under 0.9).  Raise the bed
length to 1.5\,m: $R_s$ passes 1.5, but only as $\sqrt{L}$, and the engine's
dotted peaks stay wider than the page's Gaussians --- that is its grid, one
plate per cell, and the extra row in the table accounts for it.  On the
van Deemter plot move the velocity towards $v_{\mathrm{opt}}$ and watch the
plate count rise.  Set the plate count of the plate-model panel to 3 and to
100 and compare the gap to the Gaussian.  In step 5 raise the load from 0.02
to 1.6: the peaks lean forward and come out earlier; the two solutes are
injected in equal amounts, so each lowers the other's loading and both move
forward (the first is displaced by the second, and the second is pulled
along by the first); switch competition off to see how much of that is
competition.  The tag-along plateau needs a feed dominated by the first
solute, which this page does not inject.  The table of step 6 shows the amount recovered at 99\,\%
purity rising with the load and then falling.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a longer column is the way to resolve two peaks.}  Resolution
grows only as the square root of the plate count, so twice the resolution
costs four times the column; the selectivity term $(\alpha-1)/\alpha$ is
usually the cheaper lever, and at high load no column length restores the
purity the overload took.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Liquid chromatography has no engine route: the engine's fixed bed is a gas
unit, and the overloaded column is the page's integration of equations in
the form a liquid column takes.  The velocity is held constant (a poor
assumption for a concentrated gas pulse), the band is isothermal, the
competition is the competitive Langmuir model with one saturation capacity,
the dispersion factors are $\gamma=0.7$ and $\lambda=0.5$ (the latter on the
low side of the published values, so the page's peaks are somewhat narrower
than a real column's), and gradient elution, extra-column broadening and the simulated moving bed
are not covered.

\subsection{Fluidised beds: minimum fluidisation, regimes and bubbles}\label{sec:edu-fluidised-beds}

\declaredanchor{sec:fluidised-bed}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=fluidised-beds}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none --- Choupo's engine has no fluidised-bed model, and
every number on the page is the page's own arithmetic.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have a bed of particles and a gas: at what velocity does it fluidise,
what regime will it be in, how much does it expand, and how much gas bypasses
the particles?}

The page draws the bed's pressure drop against the gas velocity: Ergun's line
through the packed bed rises until it meets the bed's weight per unit area, and
the crossing is the minimum fluidisation velocity, found by bisection and set
beside Wen and Yu's correlation with the difference printed.  It places the
powder in Geldart's groups on a straight-line approximation of the published
map, marks the velocities that bound the regimes on a velocity axis (minimum
bubbling for a group A powder, the terminal velocity of one particle from its
drag balance, and the onsets of turbulent fluidisation and of transport),
expands the bed with growing bubbles in the two-phase picture and integrates
the bubble-to-cloud exchange to show how much gas bypasses the solid, and lists
which size classes of a distribution the gas can carry out to a cyclone.  A
closing exercise asks the reader to refute three planted statements with the
page.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

The engine has no fluidised bed.  The page writes two laws in the same form as
the engine: Ergun's gradient of the plug-flow reactor's packed bed, and the
single-particle terminal velocity of the pneumatic conveyor on Schiller and
Naumann's drag coefficient.  Everything else --- the correlations of Wen and Yu,
Abrahamsen and Geldart, Bi and Grace, Mori and Wen, and Kunii and Levenspiel's
exchange coefficient --- is evaluated by the page, and every one of their
constants is listed in \file{docs/design/fluidised-bed-hydrodynamics.md} as
still to be checked against its source.  The gas is declared: its density from
the ideal-gas law, its viscosity typed.  The design record also proposes what
an engine unit for a fluidised bed would need; none is built.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
At the defaults (sand-like particles of 200\,\textmu m in air) the bed fluidises
near 5\,cm/s and is a group B powder that bubbles at once.  Change the voidage
from 0.42 towards 0.415 and watch the gap to Wen and Yu shrink, then to 0.48
and watch it grow: $\varepsilon^3$ sits in both of Ergun's terms.  Choose the
fine catalyst: a window without bubbles opens between $u_{\mathrm{mf}}$ and
$u_{\mathrm{mb}}$.  In step 4 raise the velocity and the bed diameter and watch
the bypassed share grow as the bubbles grow; compare the row with the bubbles
frozen at their size at the distributor.
\end{tryit}

\subsubsection*{The laboratory}

A button near the top of the page, \emph{Open the laboratory}, opens a second
tab in which the same arithmetic is drawn as one to three columns side by side,
one kind of particle in each, under the same gas and one velocity control.
Its task, in one sentence: \emph{I turn up the gas on a bed of particles, I
watch what the bed does and I read the instruments, and from my own readings I
find the velocity at which it fluidises and the one at which it is carried
away.}  Below the minimum fluidisation velocity the dots sit still at the
packed height; above it the bed is drawn at the page's computed height, with
voids of Mori and Wen's diameter rising at the computed bubble velocity, as
many as the computed bubble fraction asks for; above a particle's terminal
velocity the dots leave through the top and the column empties.  Each column
carries its instruments (pressure drop, bed height, regime), and a notebook
records the readings going up and coming down and plots them.  The minimum
fluidisation velocity and the terminal velocity stay hidden until the reader
types the values read off his own points; then the page's values appear, with
the difference.  The dots are a drawing, not a simulation: their number, size
and motion are not to scale, and how fast a column empties is not computed.
The kinds of particle are not mixed in one column, because the page computes
each alone and a mixed bed has rules (segregation, a mixture's minimum
fluidisation velocity) it does not have.  A cohesive group C powder is drawn
packed at every velocity, with the page's sentence on why.  The record is
\file{docs/design/fluidisation-virtual-laboratory.md}.

\begin{tryit}
In the laboratory, raise the gas slowly and record a reading at each step:
the pressure drop of the sand-like column climbs and then stops climbing
while its bed starts to swell.  Read the knee off your points, type it, and
compare with the page.  Go on to above the catalyst-like particles' terminal
velocity, watch that column empty, then come back down and record again: the
pressure drop no longer returns to where it was, because the bed has gone.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a fluidised bed's pressure drop grows with the gas flow.}  Once
the bed is fluidised the gas carries its weight and nothing more; the extra gas
goes into bubbles, and the pressure drop across the bed stays where it was.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Spherical, smooth particles of nearly one size (except in the size table); one
gas, isothermal; no wall, no distributor pressure drop, no jets; no elutriation
rate and no freeboard; no reactor model.  Every correlation is empirical, and
the published accuracies are not quoted because they have not been checked.

\subsection{When the fast obeys the slow: separation of time scales}\label{sec:edu-fast-and-slow}

\declaredanchor{ch:stiff-ode}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=fast-and-slow}.  \textit{\small
Cases it starts from:} three pairs, each a full and a reduced model, with one
declared number of the full case changed by the slider:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt]
  \item \file{unsteady07\_fast\_tank\_full} and
        \file{unsteady08\_fast\_tank\_reduced} (in \file{tutorials/unsteady/});
  \item \file{batch30\_fast\_equilibrium\_full} and
        \file{batch31\_fast\_equilibrium\_reduced} (in \file{tutorials/batch/reactor/});
  \item \file{batch32\_fast\_intermediate\_full} and
        \file{batch33\_fast\_intermediate\_reduced} (the same folder).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{My model is heavy and stiff: which variables can I stop integrating and
replace by an algebraic relation, and how wrong will I be?}

The page opens on a puzzle: a plant has more fast motions than anybody could
list, yet the formulas engineers design with have two or three variables and
fit within a design tolerance.  Why are they so simple?  The page ``Why
engineering correlations are simple'' names the separation of scales as one
mechanism and shows it on a two-variable toy; this page shows it at work on
running process models, with the engine computing both sides.  The short
answer comes first: divide the fast time scale by the slow one,
$\varepsilon = \tau_\mathrm{fast}/\tau_\mathrm{slow}$, replace each fast part
by the relation that makes its own rate zero, and the error falls with
$\varepsilon$.  Three case studies test it, each as a guess, a reveal (the
engine runs both models; nothing runs before the student presses the button),
a slider that makes the fast part slower until the reduction fails, the cost
of the full model, and ``what is left when the fast part obeys'': the reduced
law, counted against the full one, with the range of the slider where its error
stays under a tolerance the student sets.  The cases are a small tank behind a
big one (the small one replaced by a \code{storageTank} wrapped as
\code{quasiSteady}), a fast equilibrium feeding a slow step (one pool at
$kK/(1+K)$, an \code{expression} rate law) and a reactive intermediate (a rate
law in the stable species alone).  The closing section, ``So this is why
correlations are simple'', puts the three reduced laws beside the
effectiveness factor of a pellet, a film coefficient or friction factor, the
membrane module solved quasi-steady inside a tank's start-up, and the engine's
refusal of a loop with no vessel on it, quoted rather than fired.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

Every curve is the engine's: \code{choupoSemiContinuous} for the tanks,
\code{choupoBatch} with the adaptive Rosenbrock23 integrator for the reactors,
whose accepted steps the page counts from the step history the engine prints.
The page divides mole numbers by their total, subtracts the reduced curve from
the full one, and draws two closed forms as labelled checks: the largest gap of
two linear first-order parts, $\varepsilon^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}$, and the
stability limit of a fixed explicit RK4 step.  The time-integrated driver
publishes no step count, so the tank case shows its declared fixed step count
and nothing in its place.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
At the defaults the largest gaps are 4.3\,\% (tanks, $\varepsilon = 0.05$),
0.62\,\% (equilibrium, $k_f/k = 100$) and 0.95\,\% (intermediate,
$k_2/k_1 = 100$).  Move each slider to its slow end and watch the gap reach
37\,\% where the two time scales are equal.  In the intermediate case, compare
the gap with the tank case at the same $\varepsilon$: the numbers are the same,
because the two problems are the same pair of equations.  In the reactor cases
watch the step count: the stiff integrator's barely moves, while the explicit
bound grows with the fast rate.  Then set a tolerance and measure the window.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That reducing a stiff model is mainly about speed.}  With a stiff
integrator the full model costs little more; what the reduced law gains is that
it no longer needs the fast constants at all, which is why a correlation can be
fitted with two or three numbers.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Linear, first-order models only; synthetic species and author-set numbers;
isothermal reactors.  Next, named and not built: batch electrodialysis (the
film against the emptying tank) and a cascade control loop.  The measured
tables are in \file{docs/design/fast-and-slow.md}.

\subsection{How do we know $k$?}\label{sec:edu-how-do-we-know-k}

\declaredanchor{ch:gas-kinetics}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=how-do-we-know-k}.  \textit{\small
Cases it starts from:} \file{kinetics01\_oh\_decay} (a trace of OH in H$_2$/N$_2$)
and \file{kinetics02\_h2o2\_falloff} (a trace of H$_2$O$_2$ in N$_2$), both in
\file{tutorials/batch/combustion/}, with the whole \'O~Conaire 2004 mechanism
loaded.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{A rate constant in a mechanism is a number somebody measured.  How do you
isolate one reaction among the dozens that happen in a flame and read its $k$,
its activation energy and its pressure dependence from a signal?}

The page does the measurement.  The engine's batch reactor is the cell of a
flash-photolysis experiment: an isothermal closed vessel holding a bath and, at
$t=0$, a trace of a radical; the amount of the radical against time is the
signal.  With the partner in large excess the decay is first order,
$\ln c_\mathrm{OH} = \ln c_{\mathrm{OH},0} - k' t$ with $k' = k\,c_{\mathrm{H}_2}$.  Five panels
follow, each started by its own button: one decay and its fit over the first
three lifetimes; $k'$ against five concentrations of H$_2$ (the slope is $k$,
the intercept the loss that does not depend on H$_2$); the same experiment at
seven temperatures as an Arrhenius plot, whose straight-line slope is the
\emph{local} activation energy $E_a + bRT$ because the record has $b\neq0$;
the decomposition of H$_2$O$_2$ at eight pressures between the Lindemann limits,
with Troe's broadening drawn beside the points; and the two ways the method
breaks, a small excess and secondary reactions, the second shown by the
engine's own rate-of-production table.  Every recovered $k$ is compared with
the law the engine announced when it loaded the reaction; the optional noise is
seeded and labelled synthetic.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

Every signal is the engine's (\code{choupoBatch}, the Rosenbrock23 integrator).
Before each measurement the page does what an experimenter does with a pilot
run: it changes the observation window and reruns the cell until the signal
falls by three $e$-folds inside it.  The page fits straight lines to the
signals, evaluates the record's law with the units the engine announced in the
\code{[kinetics]} lines of the run's log, and draws the Lindemann and Troe
laws as labelled checks.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
At the defaults (1000\,K, $x_{\mathrm{H}_2}=0.01$, no noise) the recovered
$k$ equals the engine's to 0.02\,\%.  Switch the noise to 10\,\% and watch the
standard errors grow and the recovered $k$ move by a few percent.  In panel 3
compare the apparent activation energy with the one the record implies at the
middle of the range, and with the record's $E_a$ alone: the difference is
$bRT$.  In panel 4 move the temperature and see the knee of the fall-off curve
move to higher pressure.  In panel 5 watch the recovered $k$ climb as the
excess falls.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a straight line on a log plot proves a single reaction.}  The line
is straight when the loss is first order, not when the loss is one reaction:
with a large radical seed the secondary reactions of the mechanism feed back
and the fit still returns a number.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

The engine answers its own question (agreement with the record shows that the
method recovers $k$, not that the record is right about a real gas); no
instrument, transport or wall model; one bimolecular reaction and one
fall-off; the Arrhenius panel takes one excess per temperature.

\subsection{Assemble methane combustion}\label{sec:edu-methane-assembly}

\declaredanchor{ch:gas-kinetics}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=methane-assembly}.  \textit{\small
Cases it starts from:} \file{methane01\_h2o2\_core} to \file{methane06\_assembled}
(the ladder) and \file{methane07\_sensitivity}, all in
\file{tutorials/batch/combustion/}.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{Methane does not ignite from a handful of reactions.  Start from the
hydrogen and oxygen core in the reaction library, add the carbon chain one rung
at a time, and say, after each rung, what the engine now predicts for the ignition
delay a shock tube measured, and which reactions decide it.}

The target is one point of Petersen, Davidson and Hanson (1999, Table~2, p.~86):
mixture~2 (3\,CH$_4$ + 2\,O$_2$ + 10\,N$_2$) at 1244\,K and 114.4\,atm, measured
delay $254\,\mu$s.  The engine's batch reactor stands for the gas behind the
reflected shock: closed, adiabatic, constant volume, started at that temperature
and pressure.  The delay it prints is the time of the largest $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}t$
on the write grid, $\tau = t\,|_{\max \mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}t}$.  Six rungs, each a
shipped case whose \code{reactionSets} names the sets of its rung (the library
records of the Theory Guide section on the gas-phase reaction library, each with the journal page it was read from):
the H$_2$/O$_2$ core of \'O~Conaire et al.\ alone, where methane is a spectator and
nothing ignites; the attack on CH$_4$ (five records), where CH$_3$ appears and has
nowhere to go; the methyl step (twelve), where $\mathrm{CH_3 + O_2 = CH_3O + O}$
branches the chain and the mixture ignites at about a tenth of the measured delay;
CH$_2$O through HCO to CO (eighteen), which hardly moves the delay; CO to CO$_2$
(four), where most of the heat is released and the delay is still about a tenth of
the measured one; and the ethane route (seven), where the recombination
$\mathrm{2\,CH_3\,(+M) = C_2H_6\,(+M)}$ delays the ignition by an order of magnitude onto
the measurement.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

Every number of the ladder is the engine's: the page runs the six cases one after
another in the browser and reads the \code{tau\_ign} line of each log, the
trajectory and the final amounts.  The sensitivity is the engine's too: the
assembled case with \code{sensitivity \{ response tauIgnition; \}} integrates the
vessel once per reaction with that reaction's net rate multiplied and pins
$S_j = \mathrm{d}\ln\tau/\mathrm{d}\ln k_j$ (the Theory Guide section on the brute-force sensitivity of the delay);
the page sorts and draws the largest.  Two methyl reactions head it with opposite
signs, the branching with O$_2$ (negative) and the recombination of two CH$_3$
(positive): the delay is, to a first approximation, the outcome of that
competition, which is why a small hand-assembled set can follow a large mechanism.
The last panel is a RECORDED table, not a browser run: 31 shock-tube points of the
two nitrogen mixtures, each run under the assembled set, the C1 chain without the
ethane route, the whole Appendix A of Miller and Bowman and AramcoMech~1.3
(\file{docs/reviews/2026-11-22-methane-lesson/}).  The C1 chain alone is about a
factor of ten too fast at every point; the assembled set follows the whole
appendix to within a few tens of percent at every point and, like it, is too slow
at the lowest temperatures and highest pressures (no peroxide chemistry);
AramcoMech does not have that fault.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Run the six rungs and read the table: the ignition delay appears at rung~3 and
hardly changes at rungs~4 and~5 (the branching decides it, the later links decide
the final temperature), then grows by an order of magnitude at rung~6.  Run the
sensitivity and find the two methyl reactions.  Then read the last table against
the measured column and find the points where the assembled set is more than a
factor of three slow.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a mechanism that ignites is a mechanism that is right.}  Rung~3
ignites, at a tenth of the measured delay, for the wrong reason: a chain that
branches and has no side road to take radicals out of it.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

The carbon constants are ONE mechanism's values (Miller and Bowman 1989,
Appendix~A), not evaluated data; Baulch et al.\ (2005) and Tsang and Hampson (1986)
are the evaluated sources and are not yet transcribed in this tree.  No peroxide
or C$_2$ chemistry beyond ethane; the argon and helium mixtures of the paper are
not run; the delay is the engine's steepest temperature rise, not the
experimenters' extrapolated pressure rise.

\subsection{Operator panels and alarms}\label{sec:edu-operator-panels}

\declaredanchor{sec:operatorPanels}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=operator-panels}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} \file{batch/electrodialysis/edbatch01\_constant\_current},
cloned with at most one declared value changed for a disturbance and with the
page's own \file{system/panelDict} in place of the shipped one.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{My process publishes dozens of variables: what do I put in front of the
operator, in what form, and which alarms?}

The batch electrodialysis rig publishes 25 series at 41 written instants.
The page shows the same run twice --- every number the engine publishes, and
the six elements of the panel the case declares --- and asks the student
what is happening, first from the numbers and then from the panel, under

tasks to the least information that answers each, draws one value four ways
(a bare number, on its scale, as a trend, as a lamp), arranges overview, unit

everything that moves, grows a panel in three stages, and ends with an
exercise: a panel with five planted defects.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

Every run, and every fact about a panel, is the engine's: \code{choupoBatch}
reads the panel after the time loop and reports which limit is crossed and
when, which element never moves, which limit is never approached, which
column is not published, which alarms watch the same column, and how many
alarms are active at once.  The page writes the panels and draws the worked

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Pick the lower current efficiency and move the cursor to the end: nothing

\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That more information makes a better panel.}  Every element costs the
operator's attention, and every alarm interrupts him.  A panel that shows
everything shows nothing, and a panel where everything alarms has no alarm.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Nothing on the page is a grade.  Only the first slice of the panel exists in

The practice of alarm management and operator displays is written from
reasoning and not checked against the published standards, which were not
read; the alarm-handling rate is an assumption of the page.

\subsection{Membranes: a UF/NF batch, concentrated and washed}\label{sec:edu-batch-membrane}

\declaredanchor{ch:membrane}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=batch-membrane}.  \textit{\small
Witnesses:} \file{tutorials/batch/membrane/diafilter01\_nf\_desalting} and
its twin \file{tutorials/batch/membrane/diafilter02\_fouling\_decline},
which differ by a \dict{fouling} block and by nothing else.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have a stirred vessel behind a membrane.  I can take permeate away
and let the vessel shrink, or I can make solvent up as fast as it leaves
and hold the volume.  The first concentrates what the membrane keeps; the
second washes out what it does not.  How far does each one get, how long
does it take, and how much of the product do I lose doing it?}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

The two modes are one line of the model apart --- the diafiltrate rate
$Q_d$ is zero in a concentration and equal to the permeate rate in a wash
--- and the page makes that the mode switch.  It also makes it the
\emph{axis}: each mode is plotted against its own clock, the volume
concentration factor $\mathrm{VCF} = V_0/V$ for a concentration and the
diavolumes $N = \int Q_d\,\mathrm{d}t / V_0$ for a wash.  Neither clock is
seconds, and that is deliberate: both are dimensionless, both start at
their own origin, and both make runs at different areas and pressures
comparable, which seconds do not.  Flipping the mode therefore changes
what the horizontal axis \emph{means}, not merely the curve on it.

Four knobs re-run the engine in the browser --- the feed pressure, the
membrane area, the film coefficient $k_{\mathrm{film}}$ and the horizon
--- beside two switches, the mode and whether Hermia's cake law is
applied.  Every retained fraction is drawn on a logarithmic axis, because
the closed forms the construction is about are exponentials and a student
should be able to read a straight line where one is claimed.

\begin{tryit}
Start on the wash.  The classical result says the retained fraction falls
as $\exp\!\big[-(1-R)N\big]$: a straight line on the log axis, whose
slope is fixed by a \emph{constant} rejection.  Put the engine's curve
beside it and the two part.  Now open the rejection trace underneath:
$R_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is not constant, it climbs as the salt leaves --- and
the ideal line the page drew was built from \emph{this run's own initial}
$R$, so the gap cannot have been arranged.  Switch to concentrate mode
and watch the axis become $\mathrm{VCF}$ and the law become a power.  Watch which power: the RETAINED
fraction falls as $\mathrm{VCF}^{-(1-R)}$ while the concentration rises as
$\mathrm{VCF}^{R}$, and at $R=1$ that second form gives
$c/c_0=\mathrm{VCF}$ --- a membrane that keeps everything concentrates
exactly as the vessel shrinks.  The same assumption as the wash, spent
the same way.  Then turn
on Hermia cake and change nothing else: the twin case is the same
operation with a deposited layer, and the run buys fewer diavolumes in
the same horizon.  Finally look at the last plot --- the product
$J_w c$ against $\mathrm{VCF}$ --- and find where a wash is cheapest.
\end{tryit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Mode} --- the one line that differs, $Q_d = 0$ or
        $Q_d = Q_p$.  It changes the question, the clock and the axis.
  \item \textbf{Feed pressure} --- raises the flux and therefore shortens
        every diavolume, but it also raises the wall concentration, which
        is where the rejection's drift comes from.
  \item \textbf{Membrane area $A$} --- buys time and nothing else: the
        trajectory against $N$ or $\mathrm{VCF}$ barely moves, while the
        clock in seconds does.  That is the clocks' whole argument, made
        visible with one knob.
  \item \textbf{Film coefficient $k_{\mathrm{film}}$} --- the stirring.
        Lower it and the wall concentrates, the osmotic pressure rises and
        the flux falls, with no fouling anywhere in the case.
  \item \textbf{Hermia cake} --- a \emph{claim about a mechanism}, not a
        fitted exponent, which is why the engine makes the case state a
        \dict{reason} in writing before it will apply one.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The trade, and the one classical optimum on the page}

Washing longer buys purity and costs two things.  It costs product,
because no rejection is one and the exponent never stops running; and it
costs time, because every diavolume is a vessel volume of permeate that
must cross the membrane, $t_{\mathrm{DF}} = N V/(A J_w)$.  At a fixed
inventory of the retained species a smaller vessel is a more concentrated
one, so pre-concentrating shortens every diavolume --- and simultaneously
raises the osmotic pressure and the polarisation, which cuts the flux.
The two pull against each other, so a cheapest concentration exists, and
the classical statement of it is that it sits where the product of flux
and retained concentration is largest: Ng, Lundblad and
Mitra, \emph{Optimization of Solute Separation by Diafiltration},
Separation Science \textbf{11}(5) (1976) 499--502.

The page applies that \emph{criterion} and deliberately not the closed
form usually quoted beside it.  ``Wash at the gel concentration divided by
$\mathrm{e}$'' does not follow from the criterion alone --- it follows
from the gel-polarised flux law of ultrafiltration, and the run on this
page does not carry that law: its flux is an applied pressure less an
osmotic pressure through the declared solution-diffusion model (the
engine has the gel law as \texttt{transport gelPolarisation}, witness
\file{tutorials/steady/membranes/membrane19\_uf\_gel\_polarisation}, but this
diafiltration does not declare it).  So the tool reads the
product off the engine's own concentration trajectory, marks the maximum,
and says whether that maximum was bracketed by the sweep or sitting on one
of its ends, because an endpoint is not an optimum --- it is a run that
did not find one.  Nothing on the page borrows a constant from a model
that is not running.

\subsubsection*{The misconception the construction exposes}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the rejection is a property of the membrane.}  It is written
as a single number on a datasheet, it is taught as a single number, and
every closed form on this page is obtained by carrying it outside an
integral.  It is not a number: it is an outcome of the wall concentration,
which follows the flux, which follows the osmotic pressure, which follows
a composition that the run is deliberately changing.  A student who sizes
a wash from the datasheet's $R$ will over-wash or under-wash, and will not
know which.  The engine publishes $R_{\mathrm{obs}}$ at every instant for
exactly this reason, and the page draws the hand result from the run's own
\emph{initial} rejection so that the two curves start together and part
for a reason the student can watch.  Where they stay together, the hand
derivation was safe --- and knowing which case you are in is the whole
skill.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That flux decline means fouling.}  Lower the film coefficient in a
case with no \dict{fouling} block at all and the flux still falls, because
the wall concentrates and the osmotic pressure rises.  Concentration
polarisation is reversible and instantaneous; fouling is an accumulated
history of what the membrane has been asked to do.  The two twin cases
exist so that the difference can be seen rather than asserted:
\file{diafilter01} and \file{diafilter02} are the same operation, and one
block apart.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Said here because the page says it too, and an absence that is named is
worth more than a green run that implies otherwise.  A
\emph{feed-and-bleed membrane loop} is not a mode of this unit: it is a
steady module inside a recirculating tank, which Choupo builds as a
flowsheet --- a steady recycle at steady state
(\file{tutorials/steady/membranes/membrane18\_nf\_feed\_and\_bleed}) and, in time,
the module wrapped as \texttt{quasiSteady} beside a tank under
\texttt{choupoSemiContinuous}
(\file{tutorials/unsteady/unsteady03\_nf\_feed\_and\_bleed\_startup}).  The tree carries no
measured fouling data at all, so a cake constant is equipment-and-feed
data a case declares and never a catalogue value.  The polarisation
coefficient is declared, never correlated from a geometry the engine was
not told.  The pump work that drives the filtration is not ledgered, so
the campaign's first law reports itself \textsc{unavailable} rather than
closing on a term quietly set to zero.  And the vessel is one well-mixed
liquid at one temperature.

\subsection{The tree of chemistry: a hundred products, eleven roots}\label{sec:edu-tree-of-chemistry}

\declaredanchor{ch:balances}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=tree-of-chemistry}.  \textit{\small
Records:} the folder \file{data/standards/routes/} (its \file{README.md} is the
grammar); no engine run, and no case is needed to read the page.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{The list of commodity chemicals is a hundred names long and looks
like a hundred unrelated industries.  Is it?  And if the energy system
changes, how much of that list has to be rebuilt?}

The answer the page carries is that the list is one tree.  The products
grow from seven organic roots --- ethylene, propylene, the C4 olefins,
benzene, toluene, the xylenes, and synthesis gas with methanol as its first
derivative --- and four inorganic ones --- ammonia, sulfuric acid,
chlorine with caustic soda, and the nitric and phosphoric acids --- and the
sustainable transition replaces the \emph{roots}, not the leaves.  The
graph is a curated record: substances are nodes, a named production route
is an edge, and each edge is a balanced equation (or, for a cracker whose
feed has no formula, a stated slate) with a yield band and a citation.

\subsubsection*{Three views of one graph}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Depth.}  Pick a product.  The page walks the routes
        backwards to the roots and feedstocks and prints, on every edge, the
        kilograms of each input per kilogram of what that route makes.  For a
        reaction
        \[
          m_i = \frac{|\nu_i|\,M_i}{\nu_t\,M_t\,y},
        \]
        the floor $|\nu_i| M_i/(\nu_t M_t)$ from the balanced equation and the
        molar masses of the component records, divided by the route's yield
        $y$.  Because $y$ is a \emph{band}, so is $m_i$, and along a chain the
        bands multiply.  The page starts with three worked products ---
        ethylene and chlorine per kilogram of PVC, the Hock route to phenol
        from benzene and propylene, and methanol from CO$_2$ with the hydrogen
        from water --- and each can be changed by choosing another route for
        any substance on the path.
  \item \textbf{Convergence and forced co-production.}  Pick a root or any
        substance.  The page lists what consumes it, everything reachable from
        it, and the products at the leaves nothing else consumes.  Pick a
        route and it lists what the route makes whether anyone wants it or
        not: every product that is not its \dict{target}.  The cumene route's
        acetone follows phenol at a fixed molar ratio; a steam cracker's
        propylene, C4 cut and pyrolysis gasoline follow its ethylene in the
        proportions of a slate.
  \item \textbf{The tree of 2050.}  For each root, the incumbent routes beside
        the transition routes, with hydrogen per carbon derived from the
        stoichiometry as $-\nu_{\mathrm{H_2}}/(\nu_t n_{\mathrm{C}})$ (three
        for CO$_2$ to methanol, four for CO$_2$ to methane) and the energy
        figures where a source states one.  The ammonia edge is written once:
        green ammonia is the same reaction fed by a different hydrogen route,
        so what the page shows replaced is the \emph{feeder}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Open PVC and read the two totals.  The floor of one ethylene and one
chlorine per repeat unit is already about $0.45$ and $1.13$ kilograms per
kilogram; the bands sit above it, and the table says by how much.  Now click
the dichloroethane box and make it by oxychlorination instead of direct
chlorination.  The chlorine does not go away: the new step takes its
chlorine as HCl, which the page makes from chlorine and hydrogen, so
hydrogen and oxygen join the totals.  The record marks that route as a
declared cycle --- the balanced plant feeds it the HCl its own cracker
releases --- and the page does \emph{not} net that HCl against the
chlorine; it states the limit.  Then open phenol, go to the forced
co-production view and read the acetone beside it: one mole per mole, so
about $0.62$ kilograms per kilogram, and a single number rather than a
band, because the yield does not enter that ratio.
\end{tryit}

\subsubsection*{The numbers are estimates, and the page draws them as such}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a figure on the page was measured.}  Every yield band and
every slate share in the records is the curator's estimate
(\dict{origin assumed}, with a reason); none was read back against a
published table.  The page draws each as an estimate --- a dashed underline,
the mark ``est.'', the reason on hover and again in the detail row of the
selected route --- and says so on its first screen.  The stoichiometry is
checked atom by atom by \file{bin/curate/check\_chemistry\_graph.py}; the only
figures read from a source are the IEA's production volumes and four
ammonia energy intensities, each with its page.  A route with a Choupo case
links to it; a route without one says ``no case yet'' and links nothing.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

The graph is a selection of the chains a course on the chemical industry
names, not an inventory, and no check can see a route that is missing.  It
holds no prices, no capacities beyond the IEA's five volumes, and no
environmental inventory, so it does not say which route wins; the Choupo
cases linked from the routes, and the route-screening tool, are where that
is asked.  Co-products are listed, not credited.

\subsection{Patent expiry and generic entry}\label{sec:edu-patent-expiry}

\declaredanchor{ch:economics}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=patent-expiry}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} \file{tutorials/batch/plant/apalutamide01\_tablets}
(and, with other numbers, \file{donepezil01\_tablets}); no engine run.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{My group designs a generic tablet plant for the day the originator's
exclusivity ends.  What volume will the plant sell, at what price, year
by year, what does the case's economics read from that, and how much of
the revenue is at risk if we enter a year or two late or the price falls
further than we assumed?}

In this industry the business case is decided by a calendar, not by the
equipment.  The page takes the originator's annual sales and its price
before exclusivity ends, which fix the volume $V_0=S_0/P_0$ of the
molecule, and then applies four curves that you can read and edit: the
generic price relative to the originator's, either by the number of generic
producers or by the years since the first generic entry; the cumulative
share of the volume the generics take; the number of rivals in each year;
and the share of the generic volume the newcomer wins.  In market year $k$ the
plant sells $N=\min(V_0\,g_k\,w(n_k),Q)$ tablets at $p=P_0\,\rho\,f$, with
$g_k$ the generic share averaged over the year (the curve is read at the
\emph{end} of a year), $w$ the newcomer's share among $n_k$ producers, $\rho$ the
price ratio, $f$ the depth factor of the sensitivity and $Q$ the design production.

\subsubsection*{What the case reads}

The economics block holds one price per kilogram and one production; the
volume profile is its \code{rampUp} list, the fraction of the design
production sold in each of the first operating years
(\file{src/postProcessing/EconomicsPass.cpp} requires each entry in
$[0,1]$ and at most \code{projectLife} entries).  The page prints that
list and the one constant price $p^{*}=\mathrm{PV}/\sum_j N_j(1+i)^{-j}$ that gives the
same present value as the falling prices, in the case's own units (EUR per
kilogram of \code{packagedTablets}).  The equivalence holds for the
discount rate you gave the page; change the rate in the case and
re-run the page.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Read the table first.  The default curves bring the plant to four
producers in year three and a price of about $4.0$ EUR a tablet, the case's
own price, but the volume reaches only about ten million tablets a year,
below the fourteen million the case produces from its first year: the
case's $25\,\%$ market share and this page's curves do not agree, and the
column ``sold'' shows by how much.  Now switch the price index from
``number of producers (US, FDA)'' to ``years since first entry (EU, EC)'':
the price in year three is about $11.5$ EUR instead of $4.0$.  The two
studies differ by that much because they measure different things in
different markets, and your report has to say which one it follows.  Then
open the sensitivity table: entering one year late hardly changes the present
value at your launch (about one per cent, upwards), which hides the delay; the next
column, brought to the first generic entry, shows the loss.
\end{tryit}

\subsubsection*{The points are marked, and so are the limits}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a curve is a forecast, or that the page tells you when you may
sell.}  Each curve point says whether a published study states it
(\emph{stated}: the FDA's 2019 report for the price by number of producers,
the European Commission's sector inquiry fact sheet for the price by time
and for the generic volume in the first two years) or the page interpolated,
extended or chose it (\emph{assumed}, with its reason); a number you edit
becomes \emph{assumed}.  The number of rivals and the newcomer's share have
no source.  The databases that give price-erosion curves by product class
are commercial and are not shipped.  The page is not a freedom-to-operate
analysis: the dossier of the apalutamide case lists the European
counterparts of the patent and their supplementary protection certificates
as not verified, and the date the page starts from (3 December 2035) is
Google Patents' anticipated expiry of one US patent.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

The molecule's volume is held constant and the originator's reply
(a price cut, an authorised generic, a new formulation) is not modelled.
The sensitivities move one thing at a time on the same curves and carry no
probability.

\subsection{Ways a pharmaceutical-plant economics goes wrong}\label{sec:edu-economics-errors}

\declaredanchor{ch:economics}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=economics-errors}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none (one illustrative plant of round numbers); no
engine run.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{My group has written the economics chapter of a plant design.  Before
we hand it in, which of the mistakes that recur in such chapters are in ours,
and which of them would Choupo have caught?}

The page takes eight error classes met in earlier plant-design reports
(none is named here, or on the page) and shows, for each, the wrong
calculation and the right one on the same editable inputs, side by side.
The classes are: a sold quantity that is not the plant's production,
$N=\min(Q,Ds)$ against a quantity raised by an unargued factor; a
break-even computed on another revenue than the NPV's, tested by putting the
break-even back into the NPV; the tax charged on sales instead of on taxable
income, $T=t(S-K-d)$; a royalty charged outside the years in which a
licensed patent is in force; three working capitals (a fraction of the fixed
capital, a fraction of the sales, a build-up from days) that can differ by
more than an order of magnitude; a discount rate mistaken for the cause of
an internal rate of return, drawn as the NPV against the rate with the IRR
where the curve crosses zero; a utility bill out by a unit conversion, with
energy and volume refused as a mixture; and a cost per square metre that is
a bare number instead of a declaration with a source and a reason.

\subsubsection*{The guard under each class}

Under every example is the answer to \emph{what does Choupo do about this?},
read from the engine and quoted with the file it was read in, in one of four
words: it \emph{refuses} (the run stops), \emph{announces} (the run goes on
and prints it), \emph{constructs} (the engine's own calculation is done the
right way in one place and published, but nothing compares it with yours),
or does \emph{nothing}, and then the page says so in those words.  Choupo has
no patent calendar (a royalty is charged only inside the window the case
declares, and a window with no end is refused), computes the break-even
quantity only when the case declares it, does not choose a working-capital
method for you (it prints three side by side when asked) and does not judge
a discount rate or a unit cost.  The test of the page opens the source files it
cites and holds each guard sentence to the text it rests on, so a guard that
disappears from the engine fails the test instead of staying on the page.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
In the sold-quantity example set the factor to one and then to six: the
quantity booked goes from the market share to several times the plant's
production, and the page marks it impossible.  Switch the series to
thousands read as millions and watch the same.  In the discount-rate example
raise the rate to sixty per cent: the NPV of a sound ten-year project turns
negative, while the IRR does not move; then multiply the cash flow by six

\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a green run is a correct economics.}  The engine's chain is
consistent; it does not know whether the quantity, the price, the royalty or
the unit cost you declared are those of the plant you described.  Every
number on this page is an illustration, none is a market study, and nothing
on it is financial or legal advice.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

A year-end discounted cash flow with straight-line depreciation, no
inflation, no financing and no construction period; the engine's own appraisal
has more than that, and the page does not reproduce it.

\subsection{What a plant costs to run, and when it pays}\label{sec:edu-plant-running-cost}

\declaredanchor{ch:economics}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=plant-running-cost}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none (one illustrative plant of round numbers); no
engine run.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have the mass and energy balance of a plant and an investment.  What
does it cost to run, at what capacity does it break even, and does it pay?}

The page carries one plant through seven short calculations.  The cost of
production is itemised in four groups, direct, indirect, fixed and general,
with every factor an input and, beside it, a pointer without numbers to the
book that tabulates it (Peters, Timmerhaus and West, chapter~6): the student
reads it there and declares the value with that source.  The raw materials are the balance's quantity times a price,
never a percentage.  The same bills are then priced in Choupo's three terms,
$\mathrm{COM}_d = 0.180\,I + 2.73\,C_{OL} + 1.23\,(C_{RM}+C_{UT}+C_{WT})$,
with the difference explained term by term.  The crew is derived from the
hours a post is staffed and the hours one operator is paid; depreciation is
set by asset class against one straight line; the break-even capacity is
drawn three ways (accounting, cash, and NPV equal to zero); the cash flow
gives NPV, IRR and discounted payback with a discount rate built as
$(1+T_1)(1+T_2)-1$; a one-at-a-time sensitivity gives a tornado and the
elasticities; and the last step does the market arithmetic (price index,
currency, apparent consumption, FOB and CIF, unit margin).

\subsubsection*{What Choupo does and does not do}

Every engine statement on the page carries the line of
\code{EconomicsPass.cpp} it was read at, and the test of the page opens the
file and holds each to its text.  Choupo has the three-term cost, a crew from
a correlation or a declaration, straight-line depreciation over one life, the
cash flow with its NPV, IRR and paybacks, and a one-at-a-time sensitivity,
and, when the case declares them, a break-even (the operating one and the NPV
one, from the same revenue), a royalty charged inside a declared patent
window, a building cost from the rooms' areas priced by grade, and the
working capital by three methods side by side.  It has no itemised cost of
production and no depreciation by asset class.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Change the shifts per day from three to one and watch the people per post
fall and the labour bill with them; then change the maintenance factor and read
the NPV move.  Put the NPV break-even fraction back into the
cash flow and read a net present value of zero.  Raise the step of the
sensitivity from ten to thirty per cent and see which bar changes length
faster than the others.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a starting value is your plant's value.}  The starting values
are illustrations, and a one-at-a-time tornado ranks levers;
it does not measure risk.  The numbers on the page are illustrations, and
nothing on it is financial or legal advice.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Where to build it, what to make, and what it sells for}\label{sec:edu-site-process-price}

\declaredanchor{ch:economics}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=site-process-price}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none (invented illustrations); no engine run.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{Before I cost a plant: where does it go, which patented route does it
run, and what does the product earn at the plant gate?}

The page does the three pieces of arithmetic that the other economics pages
leave out, and ships no figure about any place, patent or market.  The
\emph{site} is chosen with a weighted matrix: the page lists prompts
(proximity to raw materials and customers, utilities, labour, transport,
climate, incentives, land), the student declares the criteria, the weights
and a score from 0 to 10 for each candidate, and the page computes
$S_i=\sum_j w_j s_{ij}/\sum_j w_j$.  It then shows how fragile the ranking
is: holding the other weights, two sites tie at exactly one weight of a given
criterion, $x^{*}=(R_a-R_b)/(s_{bj}-s_{aj})$, and the page reports, for each
criterion, the nearest weight at which the current leader is overtaken, and
plots every site's score against that weight.

The \emph{process} is chosen from patents with a method given as a checklist:
start from the patents cited in an encyclopedia entry, group them by owner,
separate industrial examples from laboratory ones, read the Examples section
for temperatures, pressures, times and yields, and check that one patent
covers the whole route.  A small calculator then turns an example (quantities
charged, product isolated, times) into kilograms charged per kilogram of
product, the process mass intensity, the E-factor, the yield against the
theoretical amount, the cycle time, the product per hour and a scale factor
to a target batch.  The page cites no patent and invents no number.

The \emph{price} is the margin chain between the shelf and the plant gate.
Each stage (a tax, a pharmacy, a wholesaler, a fixed fee) is the student's
declared input, in one of four forms that are not interchangeable: a share of
the price the stage sells at, a markup on the price it pays, a tax added on
top, or a fixed amount per pack.  The page ships no margin and no tax rate:
the instruction is to read the regulation of the market and declare each stage
with its source.  The arithmetic runs from the shelf price down to the
ex-factory price and back up from the price the plant needs, and the round
trip must return the starting price.

Beside every numeric factor the page shows the range a cited source states for
it, with that source; where no source the project has read states one, it
says \emph{range not cited} rather than quote a range from memory (at present
none of the three works the page cites states a range for these factors).

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Raise the weight of one criterion until the leader changes and read the
weight at which it did; compare with the weight the page reported.  Swap the
fixed fee with the pharmacy and watch the ex-factory price move, then swap the
tax with the pharmacy and watch it not move.
Change a pharmacy margin from a share of its selling price to a markup on its
cost with the same number and read the difference.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the matrix decides, or that a patent example is a design.}  The
weights and scores are the student's judgements and the example is one
inventor's experiment; neither is a fact about a site or a plant.  Nothing on
the page is legal advice, a market study or a freedom-to-operate opinion, and
every starting value is an illustration.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{How a batch plant is organised: lots, campaigns and the Gantt chart}\label{sec:edu-campaign-plan}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=campaign-plan}.  \textit{\small
Cases it starts from:} \code{batch/plant/apalutamide01\_tablets} and
\code{batch/plant/donepezil02\_two\_strengths}, run in the browser by a button
on the page; the two toy plants are invented and run no engine.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{My plant makes several products in lots.  Which stage paces it, how many
hours of the year does it need, where does a continuous machine such as a spray
dryer fit, and does the order of the campaigns matter?}

The page has two halves and says which one you are in.  In the \textbf{toy}
half a plant of five stages (a solution tank, a spray dryer, a blender, a
press and a coater) is redrawn as a Gantt chart while you move the lot, the
lots per campaign, the changeover, the dryer's operating time, the demand and
the number of tanks; the limiting stage is outlined as it moves, with
$T_{\mathrm{L}}=\max_j t_j/N_{\mathrm{op},j}$, each stage's utilisation and the
year's required hours against the available ones recomputed live.  A second toy
makes two or three products in single-product campaigns, with a clean at the
coating pan that depends on the direction of the change; the order is a drag,
and the page lists every order with its hours.  In the \textbf{engine} half a
button runs the case and the page draws the plan Choupo published, with the
same bars, and recomputes nothing.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says}

The toy is a second implementation of \code{BatchPlanPass.H} (lines 97--123
for one product, 205--224 for several), held by the page's test to what the
engine published for the two cases to $10^{-6}$: every cycle, $T_{\mathrm{L}}$,
offset, campaign, busy hour and Gantt bar.  With two products the year
pays each direction once whichever product goes first (\code{BatchPlanPass.H}:208--210),
so donepezil02's two trains need 880.2~h and 2079~h in either order; from three
products the order matters, and only through the directional cleans
(\code{BatchPlanPass.H}:219--220).  A semicontinuous stage that the case rates

cleaned at the campaign changeover, not between lots.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
With one solution tank the tank is outlined as the limiting stage: it is held
through the dryer's run.  Choose two tanks and the outline jumps to the dryer.
Then lengthen the dryer's operating time: the tank's cycle grows with it, and
the page warns that the plan asks the dryer for a rate it was not rated at.  In
the second toy, make the third product and reorder the campaigns: the hours
differ between the two cycles, by exactly the difference of the directional
cleans.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the cheapest order is the right one.}  It is the order with the
fewest hours; the demand, the shelf life and the safety stock decide the rest.
The engine takes the order as given and never optimises it.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

One train and no intermediate stores (a real plant decouples trains with
stores, each with its own hours), no mixed sequencing inside a campaign, no
per-product mass balances, and a clean is hours here, not a cleaning
validation (EU GMP Annex~15).

\subsection{How tall must the stack be? Gaussian plume dispersion}\label{sec:edu-stack-dispersion}

\declaredanchor{ch:stack-dispersion}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=stack-dispersion}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} \code{steady/gibbs/gibbs06\_thermal\_nox}, run in the
browser by a button on the page, whose outlet stream \code{flueGas} is read;
the toy half runs no engine.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{How tall must my stack be so that the gas it emits reaches the ground
diluted enough?  Why does a taller stack lower the ground concentration, why is
a calm clear night the bad case far from an elevated source and a sunny
afternoon the bad case close in, and how does a limit value become a stack
height?}

The page has two halves and says which one you are in.  In the \textbf{toy}
half an emission rate you set feeds four live figures: Turner's key to the
stability classes with the cell for your weather lit (day or night, sun or
cloud, wind), whose class colours the other figures; the plume in side view
with its $\pm\sigma_z$ envelope and the image source below the ground, with
sliders for the stack height, the wind, the exit velocity, the diameter and the
temperatures; the ground-level concentration along the wind on log axes, the six
classes drawn faint, the maximum marked with its distance and your limit as a
horizontal line; and a plan view of ground isopleths with a fence of the radius
you choose, so that the maximum is seen inside or outside it.  The page then
inverts the question: the least stack height for which the worst of the allowed
classes keeps the maximum under your limit, beside the good-engineering-practice
height against building downwash.  In the \textbf{engine} half a button runs
the methane burner, reads the \code{flueGas} stream (its flow, temperature,
pressure and the mole fraction of NO) and converts it to an emission rate and a
buoyancy flux for a diameter and a stack-gas temperature you set.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says}

Nothing about dispersion: Choupo has no dispersion model, no fired-unit type and
no stack as a sized item (its combustors are Gibbs reactors).  The stream is
read and never recomputed; $Q$ is the stream's NO flow times the molar mass from
the catalogue, held by the page's test to the flow the engine published.  The
dispersion is the page's own arithmetic, held by hand calculations written in
its test: the image-source slope at the ground, the normalisation of the
plume, a worked example, the closed-form maximum $2Q/(\pi e u H^{2})\,
\sigma_z/\sigma_y$ at $\sigma_z=H/\sqrt{2}$, and the inversion (the reported
height puts the maximum at the limit to one per cent).  \emph{Which coefficients
were read:} the urban sigma set and the good-engineering-practice formula; the
open-country set, Turner's table of classes and Briggs' rise constants were
written from the standard tabulation and are marked unread on the page.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Choose a sunny afternoon at 2.5\,m/s and read the ground-concentration figure:
the unstable curve is high and close to the stack.  Switch to night, clear: the
class becomes F, and far down the wind its curve rises above the unstable ones
while its maximum slides to the edge of the window.  Raise the stack height and
watch the maximum fall roughly as $1/H^{2}$; then type a limit, its source and
its date, and read the least stack height, and move the fence to see whether the
maximum falls inside it.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That the stack height the page gives is a permit.}  It is the answer of
a lesson's plume model to a limit you typed.  The limit is yours, with its
averaging time; an air-quality assessment follows the model its regulator names.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Flat ground, no terrain and no building wake beyond the good-engineering-practice
rule; no chemistry, deposition or washout (the NO of a flue gas turns into
NO$_2$ in the air); the $10$\,m wind at every height and the whole plume rise
taken at the stack; no averaging-time correction; a cold jet's momentum rise not
built; maxima outside $100$\,m to $10$\,km only flagged.

\subsection{Demand forecast for a drug product}\label{sec:edu-demand-forecast}

\declaredanchor{ch:economics}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=demand-forecast}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none (an invented illustrative history); no engine run.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{What yearly volume should our plant's economics read, how sure are we,
and how much of it is the market's and how much is our assumption?}

Three methods run on one editable history of the market, in packs or in
defined daily doses.  \emph{A}, a straight line fitted by least squares, with
the slope, the intercept, $R^{2}$, the standard error, the $t$ statistic and
$p$ value of the slope and the prediction interval of a future year
$\hat y_0\pm t_{\nu}\,s\sqrt{1+1/n+(t_0-\bar t)^2/S_{tt}}$.  \emph{B}, a dose
count $P=N\,p\,q\,m\,c\,u\,365/D_{\mathrm{pack}}$ from the population, the
prevalence, the treated fraction, the treatment share, the adherence and the
doses in a pack, reconciled against the last observed year.  \emph{C}, the
market volume times a logistic share $S(j)=S_{\max}/(1+e^{-k(j-j_{1/2})})$.

\subsubsection*{Units, and the capacity factor}

The page asks the unit of the series and of the answer (units, thousands,
millions) and computes nothing until both are said; it refuses a series
whose adjacent years differ by a factor of ten or more, which is how
thousands pasted among millions looks, unless the jump is confirmed; and it
warns when the plant and the forecast are two orders of magnitude apart.
What the plant sells is the market volume times the share it holds times a
\emph{capacity factor}.  The factor is shown as a line, together with the
factor that would fill the plant and the sold quantity as a fraction of the
whole market; a year in which the plant would have to sell more than the whole
forecast market is marked.  The factor is an assumption about the world, not a
property of the plant.

\subsubsection*{What the case reads}

The output is the pair the batch plan's economics reads
(\file{src/postProcessing/EconomicsPass.cpp}): \code{rampUp}, the fraction of
the design production sold in each operating year with each entry in
$[0,1]$ (a year the market would exceed the plant is capped at one and marked
plant-limited), the design production in kilograms through the tablets in a
pack and the mass of a packaged tablet, and \code{streamFactor fromBatchPlan}.
The price by year is not here; it is the subject of the patent-expiry tool.

\begin{tryit}
Fit the default history and read $R^{2}$ and the band: a good fit to ten years
says the line describes them, and the band says how far a new year can sit
from it.  Change the capacity factor from one to six and read the sold
quantity as a fraction of the market.  Then change one number of the series
by a factor of a thousand and watch the page refuse.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a forecast is a measurement.}  The trend assumes the cause of the
past continues, the dose count assumes five factors you estimated, and the
S-curve assumes a ceiling and a speed; none of the three is tested by any
statistic on the page.  The three disagreeing is the finding.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Only the volume is forecast, not the price; the history on the page is invented
and must be replaced by a series you can cite.

(2)~\emph{Three Jacobians}: finite differences, Choupo's audited analytic
Jacobian (default where every law has a derivative) and OpenFOAM's fast
Jacobian with the fall-off terms compiled out, and what a wrong matrix costs.

matrix is 79\,\% full and L+U is 94\,\% of dense, so sparse is only 7\,\%
cheaper, while \code{nox02}'s 17 vessels are block-diagonal and dense costs

instructions.  (4)~\emph{Four integrators} counted on \code{nox03}:
Rodas4 728 steps, 732 Jacobians and factorisations; seulex 95 steps, 92
Jacobians and 635 factorisations; Cantera's CVODES 2137 steps, 38 Jacobians,
239 factorisations; and why a big-step method wins at a tight tolerance and

per-vessel table shows seulex at $10^{-8}$ going from 20 times Cantera

day} as a lesson in method: measure before optimising, prove every speedup

\subsection*{Three ways to optimise a flowsheet \quad(\code{tool=optimizers})}\label{sec:edu-optimizers}
\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=optimizers}.  The student's question:
\emph{I have a flowsheet and a target; which optimiser can I trust, and how do
I read what it tells me?}  The page runs two toy landscapes of two variables,
declared as toys (a number of stages $s$ beside a reflux ratio $r$, a cost $f=(r+1)+1.6\,s_e$ and a
purity limit $g=0.98-P\le 0$ with the shape of the shipped case
\code{optim06\_integer\_stages}): a \emph{smooth} one, where the stage count is
real, and a \emph{stepped} one, where it is rounded to a whole stage and cost
and purity are a staircase.  The three methods are the page's own arithmetic,
stepped move by move, and the table of the real run is the engine's.

\subsubsection*{What the page shows}

Six cards and a quiz.  (1)~\textbf{The landscape}: cost bands, the limit as a
dashed line with the violated side hatched, a click to read $f$, $g$ and the
purity, and the exact optimum (a search along the limit for the smooth one, an
enumeration of whole stage counts for the stepped one).  (2)~\textbf{Nelder--Mead}:
the simplex of three points with its four moves (reflect, expand, contract,
shrink), the engine's coefficients and stop tests, and why it takes no
constraint: the driver throws on one and names the way out, a penalty term,
whose weight is the user's guess.  (3)~\textbf{SQP}: forward finite-difference
slopes, a quadratic model whose Hessian is learnt by damped BFGS, the QP of
the \code{active-set-qp} page, an L1 merit function and backtracking; at the
answer the limit is active and its Lagrange multiplier is a \emph{shadow
price}, $\lambda=\mathrm{d}f^{*}/\mathrm{d}p_\mathrm{spec}$, checked on the page
by moving the limit and re-running; on the stepped landscape the slope in
$s$ across a plateau is exactly zero, so the method converges on the plateau
it started on although the next stage count is cheaper.  (4)~\textbf{MADS}:
a mesh of size $\delta_k=\min(\Delta_k,\Delta_k^2)$, a complete poll of four
random directions (the LTMADS construction), mesh refinement after a failure
and coarsening after a success, integers on a mesh no finer than one stage,
and a progressive barrier that closes; twenty seeds are counted to show that
MADS is a local method.  The card is written \emph{from the papers} and cites the engine's own
\code{method mads;} (\code{MADS.cpp}, the driver, \code{optim07\_mads\_mixed\_column}), saying where the
page's toy differs: random against Halton poll directions, discarding against projecting onto the box, a simplified
against the papers' barrier update; the simplifications are listed under the plot.  (5)~\textbf{Reading a real run}:
the shipped \code{optim06\_integer\_stages}, one SQP per stage count from 8 to
14 (8 cannot be built with the case's feed stage, 9 to 11 stop at the top of
the reflux box, 12 to 14 converge) and its golden, $V_\mathrm{strip}=0.03479$
kmol/s at $R=2.8994$, with the multiplier of $x_{D,LK}\ge0.98$ at 14 stages,
0.83715, checked against the engine's own optimum by moving the limit.
(6)~\textbf{Which one, when}: a decision table (smooth with constraints:
SQP and its shadow prices; an integer: enumerate it; non-smooth or a black
box: MADS; a first look at an unconstrained problem: Nelder--Mead) and
McKinnon's counter-example, on which the engine's own Nelder--Mead rules
contract forever toward $(0,0)$ and report convergence at a point whose slope
in $y$ is not zero.  The same card states the \emph{no free lunch} theorem of
Wolpert and Macready (1997), read from the paper: averaged over all possible
objective functions on a finite space every search algorithm performs the same,
so there is no universal winner; and the half that keeps it from becoming the
opposite myth, that a real problem is not ``all possible functions'' but has
structure (smooth, constrained, integer, noisy), so an algorithm is good for a
class and the engineer's job is to know the class.  A table runs the page's two
landscapes: SQP is exact and cheapest on the smooth one and misled on the
stepped one; MADS is not blind to the steps but is slower and local;
Nelder--Mead does well on both toys (two variables, an adequate penalty
weight), which the page says is a property of the toys; and the quiz asks what
an engineer should conclude from the theorem.

\subsubsection*{The Bayesian section (steps 7 to 12)}

After the decision card the page carries a section on Bayesian optimisation,
the method one reaches for when a single simulation is the expensive thing.
It is a one-variable teaching sketch run in the browser, not the engine: a
Gaussian-process model with the squared-exponential kernel, conditioned on the
calls made so far (Rasmussen and Williams, Algorithm~2.1 and
eqs.~(2.25)--(2.26)), and the expected improvement of Jones, Schonlau and Welch
(eqs.~(14)--(15)), which says where the next call is most likely to pay.  A
workbench takes one call at a time on a smooth setting and on a narrow-dip
setting, with sliders for the length scale and the noise, and counts the calls
of the method against a finite-difference gradient sketch at an equal budget,
turned into wall time at 1~ms, 1~min or 1~h per call.  The narrow dip is the
honest failure: a length scale longer than the dip makes the model confident
between its points and the dip is never sampled.  The engine side is one
sentence, the measured result: an opt-in Bayesian method was built and compared
with \code{sqp} under a rule written before the runs
(the C499 protocol); on the cheap problem it
reached 1\,\% of the best value in a median of 15 simulations against 25, on
the expensive one (the reactive MTBE column) in 23 against 22, so by the rule
it is not offered.  The section's limits and four quiz questions join the
page's own, and its four sources (Rasmussen and Williams 2006; Jones et~al.
1998; Shahriari et~al. 2016; Nocedal and Wright 2006) are in the page's list.
The page's tests are \code{gui/tests/bayesOpt.test.ts}: hand-computed
Gaussian-process and expected-improvement values, the shrinking band, the loop
and the dip failure.

\subsubsection*{What the page is held to}

A test (\code{gui/tests/optimizers.test.ts}) recomputes the exact optimum
independently of every method, holds the page's SQP to it and its multiplier to
the slope of the exact optimal cost against the limit at three limits, proves
the zero slope on the staircase, reads the engine's Nelder--Mead coefficients
from \code{NelderMead.H}, checks that the four MADS directions are a maximal
positive basis of determinant $2^{2l}$, that the mesh size is the minimum of
the poll size and its square and that the barrier only falls, and reads
every cited \code{file:line} back from the tree.  The optim06 table is the
engine's run: the enumeration CSV and the run log are generated by the witness
setup and read by the test.  The typed golden numbers are held to the case's
\code{expected} file, and the engine citations of the MADS, closure and comparison-audit lines are read back like the others.  Card 6 also states that an optimiser refuses a run that breaks conservation (the driver's replay check, with ctrl16 as the example, where MADS alone had returned an objective twelve times the published one).  Sources, each verified on Crossref:
Nelder and Mead (1965); McKinnon (1998); Audet and Dennis (2006), with the
poll-direction construction read in the erratum of Audet, Cust\'odio and
Dennis (2008); Audet and Dennis (2009), whose progressive barrier is

and the problems the engine proves it on.  The original 2006 and 2009 papers

\subsection{Choosing an electrolyte activity model}\label{sec:edu-electrolyte-models}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=electrolyte-models}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none in the corpus --- the page writes its own props
cases and runs them with \code{choupoProps} in the browser.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I have a solution with salts; which activity model should I declare,
and what do I lose with the others?}

The page starts from the term every model shares, the Debye--Hückel limit
$\ln\gamma_\pm = -3A_\phi\lvert z_+z_-\rvert\sqrt{I}$ with the engine's
$A_\phi = 0.3915$ at 25\,\textdegree C, drawn alone against the measured table
of the salt the student picks.  It then writes what Davies (a fixed correction,
charge only), Pitzer (short-range virial terms per cation--anion pair, with a $\beta^{(2)}$ term in the records that carry one) and
eNRTL (a local-composition term with water and the salt as species) add, each
equation read from the source file it names.  The student picks NaCl, KCl,
CaCl$_2$ or Na$_2$SO$_4$; the page draws $\gamma_\pm$ and $\phi$ under each
model against the critically evaluated values of Hamer and Wu (1972),
Goldberg and Nuttall (1978) and Goldberg (1981) that the tree carries in
\file{data/curation/electrolytes/}, tabulates the mean and worst deviation,
turns the water activity of each model into the osmotic pressure a membrane
must overcome, asks four questions (dissolved acid gases, a second solvent, a
second salt, the ionic strength) whose answers name a model, and ends with
MgSO$_4$, for which the catalogue holds a Pitzer record and no eNRTL record.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

The engine computes every model value.  Pitzer and eNRTL run through the
package a process unit declares (\code{formulation electrolyteGammaPhi} with
\code{activityModel \{ model Pitzer; \}} or \code{model eNRTL;}) and the props
operation \code{electrolyteActivity}, one operation per molality; Davies runs
through the speciation solver (\code{speciate}, \code{activityModel davies}),
once with the network restricted to water's dissociation and once with every
equilibrium the catalogue holds for the ions --- for Na$_2$SO$_4$ that forms
NaSO$_4^-$.  Every model is asked at every measured molality, so no
deviation is interpolated.  The page computes, and prints with its constants:
the limiting law, the stoichiometric mean $\gamma_\pm$ of the run with ion
pairs from the free-ion activities the engine wrote, Davies' $\phi$ from the
engine's $a_w$, the measured $a_w$ from the table's $\phi$, the osmotic
pressure $\Pi = -(RT/\bar V_w)\ln a_w$ with the engine's
$\bar V_w = 1.807\times10^{-5}$\,m$^3$/mol, and the deviations.  Whether a
model was regressed to the table on screen is read from the record's own
\code{source} and \code{fittedInCase} lines: the eNRTL records of KCl,
CaCl$_2$ and Na$_2$SO$_4$ were, so those comparisons are marked in-sample,
and for CaCl$_2$ and Na$_2$SO$_4$ the rows the regression held out are drawn
open and scored apart.  The MgSO$_4$ eNRTL run is shown as the engine's
refusal, verbatim.  The design record
\file{docs/design/choosing-an-electrolyte-model.md} holds the measured
numbers for each salt.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
On NaCl, read where Davies leaves the table: within 0.3\,\% at
$I = 0.1$\,mol/kg and about 8\,\% at $I = 0.5$.  Switch to Na$_2$SO$_4$:
Davies on free ions is 21\,\% off already at the first row ($I = 0.3$), and the
curve with the catalogue's NaSO$_4^-$ ion pair comes back to within 3\,\% up to
$I = 0.6$\,mol/kg --- the specific interaction Davies lacks, declared as
chemistry --- and leaves again above it (+19\,\% at $I = 1.8$).  On CaCl$_2$, compare
Pitzer and eNRTL: eNRTL was fitted to this very table and still misses it by
up to 4.6\,\%.  In step~4, pick 1\,mol/kg of NaCl: Davies' water is ideal, so
its osmotic pressure is the ideal one, van~'t Hoff's law in its molality form (pure-water molar volume, not the solution's molarity).
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{A small error on a fitted table.}  An error on the points a model was
regressed to shows that the fit worked, not that the model predicts; the
page says which comparisons are in-sample.  The Pitzer records and the NaCl eNRTL
record are not regressed to a table in this tree, but their authors fitted them to
measurements of the same salts at 25\,\textdegree C, so their small errors there
also show the quality of a fit, not a prediction.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Everything is at 25\,\textdegree C, with no temperature control: 96 of the
catalogue's 99 Pitzer pair records hold their parameters at 25\,\textdegree C
only, and the single-salt eNRTL scales its two energies by $298.15/T$ and
nothing else (the engine's own simplification, not the form of Chen and Evans, 1986, eq.~45); neither is tested here away from 25\,\textdegree C.  The
measured tables start at 0.1\,mol/kg.  Only single salts in water are compared
with measurements; \code{pitzerHMW}, the mixed-solvent eNRTL and
\code{edwardsPitzer} appear only as answers in step~5.  Davies is drawn up to
$I = 2$\,mol/kg.  The osmotic pressure is the thermodynamic minimum, with the
pure-water molar volume and the bulk composition.

\subsection{Residue curve maps: azeotropic and extractive distillation}\label{sec:edu-residue-curves}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=residue-curves}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} none in the corpus --- the page writes a props case of its
own and runs it with \code{choupoProps} in the browser.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{Which products can a simple column make from a three-component feed that
has azeotropes, and why does adding a heavy solvent, changing the pressure or
splitting the liquid let a column do what it otherwise cannot?}

The page starts from the batch still of the Rayleigh page with a third
component: the pot liquid of a simple still follows a residue curve,
$\mathrm{d}x_i/\mathrm{d}\xi = x_i - y_i^{*}(x)$ with $\xi=\ln(W_0/W)$, and its
bubble temperature rises along it.  A preset (ethanol + water + ethylene glycol,
isopropanol + methanol + water, chloroform + methanol + methyl ethyl ketone,
benzene + methanol + methyl acetate, acetonitrile + ethanol + water), a pressure
and a grid are chosen; the page draws the map with arrows towards rising
temperature, the singular points (pure components and azeotropes) as unstable
nodes, stable nodes and saddles in a table with their temperatures and
compositions, the distillation boundaries out of the saddles, and the regions
they separate.  A click places a feed: its own residue curve, its region, and
the direct and indirect limiting splits on a straight mass-balance line, with
$D/F$ from the lever rule.  The page ends with the ways round a boundary (a heavy
entrainer that adds none, a heterogeneous entrainer and a decanter, a pressure
change, a recycle) and with published azeotropes beside the engine's.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

The engine computes every equilibrium number: one \code{propertyPoint} per
composition of a triangular grid asks for \code{T\_bubble} and
\code{y\_eq\_<component>} at the chosen pressure, with NRTL for the liquid (each
pair resolved from the catalogue) and an ideal-gas vapour.  The pair table shows
where the engine found each pair, its fit grade, and every sentence the engine
said about it, including each use outside its validity span.  A second run asks
again at finer compositions around each azeotrope the page located, and runs the
engine's liquid-liquid flash (\code{propertyScanTernary}, mode \code{lle}) over
the triangle at the lowest bubble temperature of the map.

The page computes the rest, and says so beside each figure: linear interpolation
inside each grid cell, fourth-order Runge--Kutta on the normalised direction of
$x-y^{*}$, the singular points (located to one grid cell, or to a tenth of
one on an edge and a quarter inside after the refinement), their type read twice --- from the boiling
temperatures around them and from the eigenvalues of
$\partial(x-y^{*})/\partial x$ --- the boundaries, the regions and the lever
rule.  It reports its own checks: how many curves start and end at a node,
whether any two drawn curves cross, and the largest fall in temperature along a
curve that the interpolation leaves.  The design record
\file{docs/design/residue-curve-maps.md} holds the arithmetic, the checks
measured on every preset, the reading of the published azeotropes, and a
proposal for an engine operation that would make the page a pure drawing.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Open the glycol preset: one region, the ethanol--water azeotrope the unstable
node and the glycol the stable node --- every residue curve runs from the
azeotrope towards the glycol: no distillation boundary blocks the split.  That
the glycol then separates the water in an extractive column is an effect of
relative volatility, which the entrainer raises; the map shows only that no
boundary stands in the way.  Switch to isopropanol + methanol + water and place a feed
on each side of the red boundary: the region, and the bottoms the direct split
can reach, change.  On chloroform + methanol + methyl ethyl ketone, change the
pressure from 1.01325 to 5\,bar and watch an azeotrope disappear from the
model's map.  On acetonitrile + ethanol + water, compare the two grids: the
model's ternary azeotrope lies a few hundredths of a kelvin below a binary one,
and the coarse grid does not agree with itself about its type.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a boundary on the map is a boundary in the plant.}  The map is the
model's: many catalogue pairs are regressions to one laboratory over a narrow
range, used here outside it, and the page prints the engine's own sentences
about each.  An azeotrope that appears or vanishes on the map may not do so in
the mixture.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

No liquid--liquid split is drawn: every bubble point is for one liquid, and the
split is checked at one temperature only.  No column is solved; the splits drawn
are the limits the rule of thumb allows, and a curved boundary can be crossed by
a straight mass-balance line.  Residue curves stand in for column profiles,
which they approximate at total reflux.  The vapour is ideal and the vapour
pressures are the catalogue's correlations, extrapolated at the higher
pressures, as the engine announces.  Measured azeotropes are compared for four
binaries only, read from measured points the corpus carries.

\subsection{Designing a column for a real mixture}\label{sec:edu-column-design}

\noindent\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=column-design}.  \textit{\small
Case it starts from:} \file{tutorials/steady/distillation/column21\_design\_curve\_ethanol\_water}
(one point of the design curve; the page reads a recorded table of the whole
curve and of the ideal control).

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{I hold a feed and two product purities.  Can a column do it, how tall,
with how much reflux, at what cost --- and how far can I trust the shortcut
that gave me the first guess when the mixture is strongly non-ideal?}

The page answers in four cards, in the order a design meets them, on two binary
mixtures at 1\,atm: ethanol/water (NRTL, with its azeotrope) and the ideal
benzene/toluene as the \emph{control}.  \textbf{Card~1, feasibility:} the
engine's boiling and condensing temperatures of the mixture, the feed and the
distillate asked for, and the lever rule $D/F=(z-x_B)/(x_D-x_B)$; past the
azeotrope the card says that no column can make the product.  The link at the
foot of the step opens the residue curve page for the ternary case.
\textbf{Card~2, the design curve:} with both key purities fixed, for each
integer stage count $N$ the reflux ratio $R$ at which the rigorous column meets
the specification; the curve $R(N)$ has two asymptotes, the minimum reflux (many
stages) and the minimum stages (large reflux), and every point is on
specification.  \textbf{Card~3, the cost optimum:} capital (the engine's Turton
total-module cost of shell, trays, condenser, reboiler and reflux drum) rises
with $N$, the heat bill falls with $R$, and their sum has a minimum; two
sliders, the price of heat and the capital charge, move it.  \textbf{Card~4, the
shortcut as a control:} Fenske--Underwood--Gilliland on the same mixture and the
same products, beside the rigorous result, with the ratios rigorous over
shortcut for both mixtures and a table for ethanol/water as the distillate is
asked closer to the azeotrope.

\subsubsection*{What the engine says, and what the page computes}

The rigorous curve is a \emph{recorded run}.  Each point is a \code{designSpec}
(two unknowns, the reflux ratio and the distillate rate; two targets, the
column's own \code{x\_D\_LK} and \code{x\_B\_HK}) around a
\code{model simultaneous} column, about twenty MESH solves, so the page does not
repeat it in the browser: \file{bin/curate/c501\_column\_design\_table.py} runs
\code{choupoSolve} once per stage count and writes the table the page reads
(\file{gui/src/case/columnDesignRecorded.json}); the case above reproduces any
row by changing \code{nStages} and \code{feedStage}.  The recording also holds
the engine's tray-hydraulic diameter, its Turton costs, the reboiler and
condenser duties and utility prices, the shortcut column's Fenske, Underwood and
Gilliland answers on the same products, and the engine's own boiling and
condensing temperatures.

The page computes the lever rule, the annualised cost $C_A=f_c C_\mathrm{inst}
+ h\,p_E Q_\mathrm{reb} + C_\mathrm{cool}$, the position of its minimum over the
recorded stage counts, and the ratios; it says so beside each figure.
Where the azeotrope is drawn it is read as the crossing of the engine's bubble
and dew curves on a scan of thirty compositions, interpolated linearly.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
On ethanol/water drag the distillate purity past the azeotrope: the card turns
from reachable to unreachable, and no number of stages repairs it.  Switch to the
ideal control and every purity is reachable.  In card~2 read the two asymptotes
and notice that the stage count at which the curve leaves the axis is far from
the shortcut's Fenske value on the real mixture and close to it on the control.
In card~3 raise the price of heat and watch the star move to more stages and a
smaller reflux; then raise the capital charge and watch it move back.  In card~4
compare the two ratios of the control and of ethanol/water, and read the last
table downwards: the nearer the distillate is asked to the azeotrope, the larger
the shortcut's error.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{Reading the cost optimum as a price.}  The shape and position of the
minimum are the lesson; its depth depends on a capital charge, a heat price and
operating hours that the page states and cannot know.  And the rigorous minimum
reflux on the page is the recorded curve's own floor, an upper bound of the true
$R_\mathrm{min}$, not a pinch calculation.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Two binary mixtures stand for the general case: no ternary, no entrainer, no
side draws.  The recorded column is the simultaneous one with constant molar
overflow and an ideal-gas vapour.  The feed enters at a fixed fraction of the
column, a little above the best feed stage at every $N$.  The exchanger areas

\subsection*{When do I open the press? \quad(\code{tool=filter-press-cycle})}\label{sec:edu-filter-press-cycle}
A plate-and-frame press filters at constant pressure and every cycle ends with a dead time $t_d$ for discharge and
cleaning, so the \emph{average} rate over a whole cycle, $R = V/t_c$, has a maximum, and the question is at what cake
size to open the press and how much a wrong choice costs.  Five steps and one card, on the bundled case
\code{filter02\_caco3\_pressWashing}, run in the browser.  Ruth's law gives the filtration time
$t_f = K_pV^2/2 + BV$ with the engine's own $K_p$ and $B$ (\code{CakeFilter.cpp}:336--338); the cycle adds the
engine's washing time $t_w = (w/f)\,V\,(K_pV + B)$ (the wash volume is a fixed fraction of the filtrate and is
pushed at a quarter of the final rate, lines 361--373) and the declared dead time (line 405).  Collecting terms,
$t_c = aV^2 + bV + t_d$ with $a = (\tfrac12 + w/f)K_p$ and $b = (1 + w/f)B$, and $dR/dV = 0$ gives
$aV_*^2 = t_d$ and $R_* = 1/(2aV_* + b)$; for no wash and $B = 0$ this is the textbook rule, the filtration time
equal to the dead time.  Measured as a multiple $x = V/V_*$ the rate is
$R/R_* = (2+\beta)x/(x^2 + 1 + \beta x)$ with $\beta = b/(aV_*)$, which is unchanged by $x \to 1/x$: opening at half
the best volume and at twice it lose exactly the same share.  The card moves the dead time (a slider, minutes) and
draws $R$ against $V$ whole, with $V_*$, the 95~\% line, the case's own cycle, and \emph{six engine runs} of the case
(its own cycle and five other volumes, at the slider's dead time) sitting on the curve; a table lists, for
$V/V_*$ from $1/3$ to $3$, the filtration, wash and cycle times, the rate, its share of the maximum, the cake
thickness and the presses the feed needs.  On the case as shipped ($t_d = 20$~min, $V = 3.37$~m$^3$) the engine's
$K_p = 37.72$~s/m$^6$ and $B = 16.01$~s/m$^3$ give $w/f = 0.4$, a best volume of 5.95~m$^3$ per cycle and a best
rate of 8.45~m$^3$/h against the case's 7.30~m$^3$/h (86~\% of the maximum); without washing the classical rule
puts the best volume at 7.98~m$^3$.  Limits stated on the page: a cake of constant resistance and a cloth that does
not clog, constant pressure, one declared dead time that does not depend on the cake, and no check of the cake's
thickness against a frame.  References: Ruth (1935), Geankoplis, Hersel and Lepek (5th ed., Section~24.2),
McCabe, Smith and Harriott (7th ed.).  Theory: Section~\ref{sec:cakeFilter-batchPress}.

\subsection*{Which dryer for which product \quad(\code{tool=dryer-selection})}\label{sec:edu-dryer-selection}

test of the sample (critical moisture, the falling-rate curve, the gas-film coefficient, the sorption constants)
against those the plant decides, each with the number the bundled case declares; then the three flow
arrangements, and last, in a collapsed drawer, the selection tree and the tables of the books, none of them removed.
A \emph{selection} aid: the seven dryer types that do most of the drying ---
spray, direct-heat rotary, fluidised bed, tray, belt, drum and freeze ---
each drawn as a schematic made for the page (wet feed and wet material in
blue, hot gas in red, product in amber, vapour in grey, a cold surface in

and answers with one or two types, the reason, and the book and page it was

Drying}, 3rd ed.\ (2006), Chapter~1 (Tables~1.1--1.6, section~1.5.3);
Perry's \emph{Chemical Engineers' Handbook}, 9th ed.\ (2019), Section~12
(Table~12-11, the selection considerations on p.~12-36); van~'t Land,
\emph{Drying in the Process Industry} (2012), whose two decision trees
(Figs.~3.1 and~3.2) supply the 30 and 75~$^\circ$C product-temperature bands
(the second reaches the spray dryer only together with ``particle size

Towler, \emph{Chemical Engineering Design} (2008), Table~10.7.

After an expert review the drawings were checked against the chapter of
Mujumdar's Handbook that describes each equipment: the rotary shell is inclined
so that the feed enters the upper end and the product leaves the lower end,
with a burner and combustion chamber at the feed end and a cyclone on the

on its surface (Chapter~9).  A test reads the rotary drawing's transform and

co-current note is attached only to the spray and rotary picks; a well-mixed

\paragraph{Co-current, counter-current, cross-flow.}  A closing section (steps
9 to 12) is about the one choice of a continuous convective dryer that the
types above do not make: which way the gas meets the solid.  It first says it
in plain words --- the gas is hot and dry where it enters and cool and humid
where it leaves, so where it enters decides what each part of the solid sees:
the wettest solid meets the hottest gas in co-current flow, the driest solid
meets it in counter-current flow, and every length of the solid meets gas fresh
from the inlet in cross-flow --- with one original schematic for each (solid and
gas drawn from primitives in the page's colour code; the gas is red where hot and
grey where used up, and a test reads each arrow's direction from its own
coordinates).  It then states the balances as the Theory Guide section
\code{sec:dryer-flow-arrangements} does and as \file{ConvectiveDryer.H} and
\file{ConvectiveDryer.cpp} implement them (the water balance that makes the
gas humidity algebraic in the solid moisture, so that there is one differential
equation $S\,\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}\xi=-RA$; the shooting on the outlet moisture
that counter-current flow needs; the log-mean element of cross-flow), each
engine line cited by file and line and held to its text by the page's test.

has passed, and, from the same runs' KPIs, a table of $X$ out, $Y$ out, $T$ air

\subsection*{How a plant tells you something is wrong \quad(\code{tool=fault-diagnosis})}\label{sec:edu-fault-diagnosis}
\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=fault-diagnosis}.  No engine is run and no
case is opened: every number is computed in the page, from assumptions the
page declares, and every plant is a toy.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{A plant behaves oddly.  Which measurement would tell the possible
explanations apart, what should I compare it with, and what does a given
disagreement prove and not prove?}

The page opens with a story that happened in the author's house.  A home
dehumidifier showed a dip of 0.5 to 0.7~$^\circ$C in the room temperature
every 4.5~h, with humidity spikes; the first guess was auto-defrost; the cause
was that a full water tank stopped the unit for about 20~min while a slow pump
emptied it, so its own heating stopped and the house, which stores heat, took
longer to recover.  Its on/off band of 50 to 55\,\% relative humidity was so
narrow that its sensor, sitting in the unit's own warm air, mostly read the
unit's heating and not the moisture; and the sensor read about 15 points high,
found only by comparing in \emph{absolute} humidity with a second, independent
sensor (18.4~$^\circ$C and 68\,\% against 22.3~$^\circ$C and 52\,\%, which are
10.70 and 10.27~g/m$^3$ on the page: the same air).  Setting the unit to 35--40\,\%
dried the house.  Four lessons are drawn from it: pick the observation that
\emph{separates} the hypotheses (the length of a stop and the response to a
change of load, not the period both explanations share); diagnose with
\emph{conserved} quantities (the mass of water) and not with state-dependent
ones (relative humidity); redundancy exposes a biased sensor; and a controller
can measure the wrong thing.

\subsubsection*{What the page shows}

Thirteen steps, each with \textbf{one original diagram} drawn from rectangles,
lines and text (no image from anywhere) and, for the methods, a live figure.
After the story: limits and alarms (warning and alarm limits, the trade
between a false alarm and a late one); the \emph{residual}, measured minus the
model's prediction, on a tank whose balance accuses a pair of flowmeters but
not which one; a heat exchanger whose fouling is a slow drift of the estimated
$UA$ while the hot and cold duties still agree, against a shifted thermometer,
a step that breaks that agreement; a pump whose worn operating point drops
below its catalogue curve while a closed valve only moves along it (the figure
finds the valve that gives a healthy pump the worn pump's flow, so the flow
alone cannot tell them apart); two sensors, and a vote of three; \emph{data
reconciliation} by weighted least squares, derived in five moves on a
three-stream splitter, with the global chi-square test and the measurement
test.  On the splitter's single balance every meter gets the same score and the
test cannot choose; a mixer added downstream puts the middle stream in two
balances and the culprit stands out, while two meters that always appear
together stay indistinguishable, which the page says.  Then the four \emph{time
signatures} (step, drift, oscillation, period) with a small classifier whose
three numbers are printed, and control charts: three-sigma limits, the
average run length $1/p$ (about 370 points between false alarms), and a note
that multivariate statistics (Hotelling's $T^2$, PCA and PLS) exist for plants
with many variables.

\subsubsection*{The challenges}

The last section is a set of \textbf{generated challenges}: a small plant (a
tank, an exchanger, a flow loop, a draining tank with a pump, a thermostat)
with a hidden, randomised fault or none --- a flowmeter bias, a drift, fouling,
a shifted thermometer, a stuck valve, a slow pump, a controller band too
narrow --- of which the student sees only the logged measurements.  A tick box
computes the residuals and signatures the methods above use.  The student
names the fault and the observation that proves it; the page checks both and
shows the diagnoser's reading.  The same seed always gives the same case, and
about one in eight is healthy.  The eight numbered buttons give each fault once,
easy to hard; any seed can be typed.

\subsubsection*{What the page is held to}

The saturation pressure of water is the Wagner equation with the coefficients
of \code{data/standards/components/water.dat}, and a test reads that record;
the chi-square and normal probabilities are checked against exact cases
(two degrees of freedom is an exponential law) and against the three
probabilities Seborg prints (0.6827, 0.9545 and 0.9973); the run lengths are
Montgomery's $1/p$; the reconciliation is checked against a hand calculation
and its least-squares optimality; and the challenge generator is pinned by seed
and shown \emph{solvable from the data it prints}: a rule-based diagnoser, which
reads only what the student reads, names the hidden fault on 3000 seeds.
Sources, read at their pages: Seborg, Edgar, Mellichamp and Doyle,
\emph{Process Dynamics and Control}, 4th ed.\ (2017), p.~163 and Chapter~21
(pp.~397--398, 407--408); Montgomery, \emph{Introduction to Statistical Quality
Control}, 6th ed.\ (2009), p.~191 and pp.~400--401.  The diagnosis literature
(Venkatasubramanian et al., \emph{Computers and Chemical Engineering}, 2003;
Chiang, Russell and Braatz; Isermann; the Tennessee Eastman problem) is named
as further reading only: it was not read for the page and no number is taken
from it.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
In the room figure make the stop 0~min and then 40~min and watch the dip.  In
the separation figure double the moisture load: the defrost timer's period does
not move and the tank's halves.  In the humidistat figure raise the sensor's
self-heating from 0 to 2.5~K and count the switchings (8, 14, 32 and 73 in
8~h at 0, 0.8, 1.5 and 2.5~K).  In the reconciliation figure put the error on
F3 with the mixer and then on F1: F1 and F2 tie, F3 does not.
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a test that fires has found the fault.}  A balance accuses the
meters in it; one balance cannot choose among them; two sensors cannot say
which one is wrong.  Naming the fault takes one more independent piece of
information, and some faults cannot be told apart from the measurements at
hand.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

Every plant is a toy with assumed numbers and generated noise: no real plant
data, no engine run, and the thresholds (six standard errors, thirty
switchings an hour) are teaching values, not plant settings.  Multivariate
monitoring, model-based observers and learning-based diagnosis are named, not
built.

\subsection*{Where to put a control loop \quad(\code{tool=loop-placement})}\label{sec:edu-loop-placement}
\textit{\small Tool:} \code{tool=loop-placement}.  The engine runs: three
gain runs and four wirings of the shipped two-loop reactor
\code{tutorials/ctrl/ctrl22\_reactor\_level\_and\_temperature} (\code{choupoCtrl},
in the browser).  The page counts valves and does the $2\times2$ arithmetic.

\subsubsection*{The question}

\emph{A vessel has several things to hold.  How many can I hold, which first,
and which valve should hold which variable?}

\subsubsection*{What the page shows}

Six steps.  (1)~\textbf{Count the valves}: the control degrees of freedom
$N_{FC}=N_F-N_D$ are the independent manipulated inputs, so two valves in
series on one pipe count once (Seborg et al., Sec.~13.1); a counter lets the
student fit valves and list wishes.  (2)~\textbf{Hold the inventory first}: the
level integrates the flow imbalance, and the page runs the wiring \emph{without}
the level loop, which the engine refuses with an overflow rather than clamping
the level.  (3)~\textbf{Measure the gains}: three engine runs (a base, the
jacket up by 2~K, the feed up by 2\,\%), the level loop closed, give the
steady-state gain matrix $K_{ij}=\Delta y_i/\Delta u_j$ of the temperature and
the product's mole fraction against the jacket and the feed rate; nothing is
typed.  (4)~\textbf{The relative gain array}: Bristol's
$\lambda=1/(1-K_{12}K_{21}/K_{11}K_{22})$, rows and columns summing to one,
computed from those gains; here about 1.9 for temperature on the jacket and
composition on the feed, and about $-0.9$ for the swap.  (5)~\textbf{The wrong
pairing, run}: the same recipe on both wirings; the recommended one reaches both
set points, the swapped one drives the feed to its lower limit and misses the
temperature by tens of kelvin, and the same happens at half and twice the loop
gain.  (6)~\textbf{Inventory, then throughput, then quality}: with the feed
fixed the third valve holds one quality and the other floats, and the run
reports where.

\subsubsection*{What the page is held to}

A test re-runs every number natively: the array against Seborg's Example~18.3
closed form ($\lambda=x$), the two routes to the array equal, the signs of the
gains, the swapped wiring's failure at three loop gains, the overflow
refusal, and each \texttt{file:line} the prose cites (\code{DynamicCSTR.cpp},
\code{VariableVolumeHoldup.cpp}, \code{PIDController.cpp}).  Sources, read at
their pages: Seborg, Edgar, Mellichamp and Doyle, \emph{Process Dynamics and
Control}, 4th ed.\ (2017), Sec.~13.1 (pp.~230--231), Sec.~13.2.1 Guideline~1
(p.~232) and Sec.~18.2 (pp.~332--335); Bristol's 1966 paper is cited by that
book and is not in the library.  The ordering ``inventory, then throughput,
then quality'' is a design reading: the first move has the Design Guide and
Guideline~1, the middle move has no source and the page labels it so.

\subsubsection*{What to vary, what to watch}

\begin{tryit}
Fit a second valve in series on the feed line and see the count stay at three;
then add ``a set product flow'' to the wishes and see one too many.  Run the
swapped pairing and read the feed rate on its lower limit, then run the
recommended one and read what the composition loop did to the feed rate (it
rose by about 16\,\%).
\end{tryit}

\begin{pitfall}
\textbf{That a positive relative gain means no trouble.}  Above one the other
loop weakens this one (the closed-loop gain is the open-loop gain divided by
$\lambda$); the recommendation is ``positive and close to one'', and 1.9 is
the first, not the second.
\end{pitfall}

\subsubsection*{What this tool does not carry}

The array is steady-state arithmetic and says nothing of speed; the gains are at
one operating point (the relative gain moves from 1.9 to about 2.0 or 1.8 with
five times the step); the reactor is a toy and the PI recipe (half a unit of loop
gain, integral time 300~s) is the page's assumption, the same for every
pairing; controlling a composition with the feed rate is unusual and is here to
make the system square; the array is $2\times2$ and the level, closed in every
run, is not in it.  The Theory Guide has no section on pairing; the link opens
the dynamic reactor chapter whose controller contract the runs use.
\declaredanchor{ch:dyn-cstr}

\subsection*{Pump against system curve \quad(\code{tool=pump-system})}

\subsection*{Why does a correctly sized valve still control badly? \quad(\code{tool=control-valve})}\label{sec:edu-control-valve}
A control valve is sized once, at one flow, and then asked to work over all of
them.  The page runs the engine's pump and pipe
(\code{pumpSystem01\_operating\_point}, swept over the flow, in the browser) and
reads the pump's rise and the line's drop at a rated flow you choose; the
valve's \emph{authority} $a$, the share of the available pressure it holds wide
open, comes from those two numbers and is never typed.  Seven cards.
(1)~\emph{A valve is a resistance in series}: the pump's rise stacked as static
elevation, line and valve, re-split as you move the opening; the engine's pump
rise and line loss at the rated and the minimum flow are in a table, with the
constant-power pump's rise at the minimum flow printed against the constant
source head the toy assumes.  (2)~\emph{The inherent characteristic}: linear,
equal percentage ($R$ declared) and quick opening, drawn as conventions of valve
makers, with no source cited.  (3)~\emph{Installed}: the installed flow
$q=f/\sqrt{a+(1-a)f^2}$, derived in four lines on the page, over the inherent
promise, with the opening below which it is more than a tenth above it.
(4)~\emph{The loop gain}: $\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}x$ for the three shapes, the
largest and smallest gain and their ratio printed (the page ranks nothing), and
the gain margin that ratio leaves.  (5)~\emph{Rangeability}: one big valve
against a running plus a starting valve, with the opening each sits at at the
minimum load and the installed turndown; the two opposed pieces of advice are
put as a question.  (6)~\emph{Choking and cavitation at small flow}: the valve
drop against the flow, largest where the flow is smallest, with the letdown
page's own choked drop and cavitation index and the water's vapour pressure
from a \code{choupoProps} run at your temperature.  (7)~\emph{Which way it
fails}: six services, authored consequences for fail open, closed and in place,
marked \emph{authored, not computed}.  The characteristics, $R$, the lowest
controlling opening, $F_L$ and the starting valve's size are the student's or
the page's declared values, marked as such.
\declaredanchor{sec:control-valve}

\subsection*{Which compressor, and how far can I turn it down? \quad(\code{tool=compressor-choice})}\label{sec:edu-compressor-choice}
A compressor is chosen from the gas as it enters the machine, a volume flow, and
a pressure ratio, not from kilograms and kilowatts.  The page runs two sized
compressors of the engine in the browser (the air machine of
\code{compressor03\_staged\_design} and the make-up compressor of
\code{ammonia02\_full\_plant}) and reads their design sheets.  Five cards.
(1)~\emph{The duty is a volume and a ratio}: the two duties side by side, and the
air machine's volume and ratio handed to the other gas, which moves the mass flow
and the head per kilogram but, because the molar mass cancels from
$W=P_1Q(r^{m}-1)/(m\eta_p)$, hardly the power.  (2)~\emph{Where each family can
exist}: the machine chart the engine selects from (Brown 2005, Fig.~1-2), drawn
from the \emph{engine's own export} in the design sheet, never from corners typed
on the page, with the duty as a point you drag; the machines whose rectangle holds
it light up, the engine's pick is starred and its tie-break (the order of its
list; the book gives none) and its two fall-backs are stated; the four compressors
of the reference ammonia plant are marked from a \emph{recorded} run, labelled as
such.  (3)~\emph{Seven questions the chart does not answer}: a grid for four
machines, \emph{authored, not computed}, with a tally over the questions you
tick.  (4)~\emph{The highest pressure the machine can make}: the driver's limit
for a positive-displacement machine, a shut-off head that is a multiple you set
for a centrifugal one, the vessel's design pressure you set, the verdict, and a
link to the Overpressure page for the relief.  (5)~\emph{The anti-surge valve is
not free}: the engine compresses the total flow the machine must pass, cooled and
with the recycle returning hot, and the page prints power, discharge temperature
and energy per kilogram delivered against the design.  The engine holds no
characteristic map, so the surge flow and the shut-off head are the student's
declared values; surge itself, speed control, suction throttling and cylinder
unloading are not on the page, and it says why.

\subsection*{From the calculated to the bought \quad(\code{tool=calculated-to-bought})}\label{sec:edu-calculated-to-bought}
A calculation gives a minimum specification and a purchase gives a size.  The
page runs the engine's own condenser design with a margin you set and a stock
list of tube lengths and shells that the witness declares
(\code{hxBought01\_margin\_and\_stock}, in the browser), and reads the three
areas it prints side by side: the \emph{calculated} one, the one \emph{required
with the margin}, and the \emph{bought} one, which is also the area the cost is
priced on.  Six cards.  (1)~\emph{A calculation gives a minimum; a purchase
gives a size}: the three areas as bars, the tubes, the length and shell bought
and the purchased cost, at the margin you move from nothing to thirty percent.
(2)~\emph{A margin is a decision about an uncertainty you can name}: the engine
is swept over the margin and the page marks the cheapest swept margin whose
bought area still delivers the duty if the overall coefficient falls by the
fraction you set ($A\ge A_\mathrm{calc}/(1-u)$, the page's arithmetic on the
engine's areas).  (3)~\emph{The stock steps}: one engine run per stock length
at your margin, the length with the smallest installed area in bold.
(4)~\emph{A pipe: the next nominal size up}: the engine's line list for the
pumped-drum case with the velocity ceiling of the liquid window moved by you,
and the smallest bore that ceiling allows ($d_\mathrm{min}=d\sqrt{v/v_\mathrm{max}}$,
the page's arithmetic from the engine's own bore and velocity) beside the size
bought; only the 4~inch line is priced, and the page says so.
(5)~\emph{A driver}: the staged compressor's shaft power and the rating the
engine printed at its margin, the engine's own note on that margin quoted from
the run's log, the rating at a margin you set and a frame ladder you declare

cost against the margin in steps, with the sentence that a margin is chosen for

assumption, not a maker's catalogue; the page predicts no engine number.

\subsection*{Compressing a gas \quad(\code{tool=compression})}\label{sec:edu-compression}
Why one stage cannot compress a gas from 1 to 12~bar, why the pressure ratios
of a staged machine come out equal, and what the real gas adds, worked on the
engine's own staged design.  The page starts from the polytropic head of
Brown (2005, Eq.~2.73) and the discharge temperature $T_2 = T_1
r^{(n-1)/n}$ (Eq.~5.16), separates the isentropic efficiency from the
polytropic one (the same 0.75 is 0.719 at the engine's section ratio and
0.656 over the whole ratio of 12), turns a temperature limit into a ceiling
on the ratio of one section, and \emph{derives} that $N$ sections cooled
back to the suction temperature take the equal ratio $R^{1/N}$ (a Lagrange
multiplier on the logarithm of the product of the ratios; Brown gives the
two-section result, Eqs.~3.14--3.17).  The student moves the number of
sections (one to four) and the intercooler temperature, and reads the total
shaft power, each section's discharge temperature against the declared limit
and the work saved against a single section; at the engine's own design
(three sections of air cooled back to 298.15~K, 1281.9~kW) the page's total
is the engine's, and a test holds the page's section~1 (412.44~K, 87\,036~J/kg,
427.31~kW) to the specification sheet to the digits it prints.  The last step
runs the make-up compressor of the reference ammonia plant (hydrogen and
nitrogen, 25 to 245~bar, two sections) and shows the compressibility and the
exponent the package contributes: the head is $Z_\mathrm{avg}$ times the
ideal-gas head, 2.3\,\% more in the first section and 7.5\,\% more in the
second.  Away from the engine's own design the page holds the exponent of
section~1 for every section, which it says; it sizes no machine, no frame, no
surge margin and no intercooler as an exchanger.  Witnesses:
\code{tutorials/steady/rotating/compressor03\_staged\_design} and
\code{tutorials/plant/ammonia02\_full\_plant}.
\declaredanchor{sec:compressor-design}

\section{Using a tool in a lesson}\label{sec:edu-lesson}

Three patterns that work, drawn from how the tools are built rather than
from how they could be used.

\subsection*{Turn one knob, and predict first}

Every classroom tool re-solves in the browser on each knob change, which
makes the interesting move cheap: ask for a prediction, then turn the
knob.  The productive knobs are the ones whose consequence is
counter-intuitive --- $\Delta T_{\min}$ on the pinch tool, area on the
$\varepsilon$--NTU chart, \code{T\_guess} on the Van~Heerden diagram, the
solute loading on the Kremser tool.  All four are documented above with
what should happen.

\subsection*{Make the deviation chip the discussion}

Several tools put a classical hand method beside the engine's answer and
report the gap.  That gap is the lesson, and its size is not the point ---
its \emph{cause} is.  On the Kremser tool the gap is the isothermal
assumption failing, and it grows the more exothermic the duty.  On the
cooling-tower tool the gap is nearly zero, and saying so plainly rather
than manufacturing drama is itself worth modelling for students: a hand
method that reproduces a converged integral to five figures has been
\emph{validated}, and that is a result.

\subsection*{Read the refusal}

An engine that refuses is teaching.  A pinched McCabe--Thiele
construction, an infeasible cooling tower, an absorber whose stripper twin
does not activate the tool, a drying run that never reached its critical
moisture --- each of those is a message with a reason in it.  The tools
show the engine's own words verbatim rather than a generic failure, and
the reason is usually the physics of the case rather than a mistake in the
input.

\begin{keyidea}
All models are wrong; some are useful.  Everything in this plane is built
so a student can see \emph{which} assumption is doing the work --- and
what happens on the day it stops being true.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Notes for maintainers}\label{sec:edu-maintenance}

\subsection*{One anchor, and it must resolve}

Each tool's registry entry names a Theory Guide destination.  The preamble
shared by all the manuals sets \code{destlabel=true}, so every
\code{\textbackslash label} in the Theory Guide becomes a PDF named
destination and the book icon jumps straight to it.  An anchor the guide
does not define fails \emph{silently} in a browser --- the PDF simply
opens at page one --- so a test in the application checks every declared
anchor against the guide source.  A tool without a resolvable anchor is
not finished.

\subsection*{Anchors are chapter-level, and some chapters are broader than
their tool}

The declared anchor points at the section that owns the tool's \emph{unit
operation}, which is not always the section that derives the tool's
\emph{construction}.  Where the two differ, the \emph{Where the derivation
lives} paragraphs above name both destinations rather than pretending the
first covers everything.  Two cases are worth knowing about before adding
a seventh tool section:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item A construction may be the hand method for a chapter that derives
        the \emph{rigorous} model instead --- the staircase against the
        stagewise column, the Levenspiel areas against the differential
        balance.
  \item A construction may be argued most fully in its \emph{witness
        case's} own dictionary header rather than in the manual.  That is
        a real source and this guide cites it as one, but it is not a
        substitute for a derivation, and a section that has to reach for
        it is flagging a gap in the Theory Guide rather than filling one.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{When a tool changes}

A knob added or removed, a chip relabelled, a witness re-parameterised:
each of those can invalidate a \emph{What to vary, what to watch} block
here, and none of them will fail a build.  The blocks are written to name
knobs by their on-screen labels so a mismatch is at least visible to a
reader with the tool open.  Prose staleness is deliberately not gated in
this project --- a text search cannot tell a live claim from a recorded
one --- so the check is human, and it belongs in the same change that
touches the tool.
